\title{Temperature Fragility and the Conditional Benefits of Truncation Sampling}
\author{Anonymous}% double-blind: fill after acceptance

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Large language models generate text by sampling each token from a predicted distribution, and a
temperature parameter sets how far the draw strays from the most probable tokens. Truncation
samplers such as top-$p$ and min-$p$ discard the least probable tokens before the draw, so that
sampling at high temperature stays coherent. Their reported accuracy gains come from temperatures
of 1.5 to 3, while the defaults of deployed systems cluster between 0.6 and 1.0. Whether they change
accuracy at those defaults, and for which models, has not been measured. We test thirteen open-weight
models on GSM8K and MMLU-Pro at temperatures 0.7, 1.0, and 1.3 in one controlled pipeline, seven
of them under eight decoding configurations. Six of the thirteen models lose 17 to 38 accuracy
points on MMLU-Pro between 0.7 and 1.3, and the other seven lose at most 10. The lost accuracy
comes from generations that run to the token limit or never state an answer. These results suggest
that truncation samplers improve accuracy primarily when higher temperatures substantially degrade
model performance. Where accuracy remains stable across temperatures, none of the tested
truncation samplers improves on plain temperature sampling.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Sampling is the default way deployed language models generate text \citep{shi2024decoding}.
At each step the model assigns a probability to every token in its vocabulary, and the decoder
draws one from that distribution. A temperature parameter reshapes the distribution before the
draw. Low temperatures concentrate probability on the few most likely tokens. High temperatures
spread it toward the tail of the distribution, the many tokens the model rates as unlikely, where
its estimates are least reliable \citep{holtzman2020curious,hewitt2022truncation}. The typical
defaults of model vendors and inference engines cluster between 0.6 and 1.0
\citep{renze2024temperature,hochlehnert2025sober},\footnote{\texttt{generation\_config.json}
of \texttt{meta-llama/Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct} sets 0.6; llama.cpp's \texttt{common/sampling.h}
sets 0.8; the OpenAI chat-completions and Anthropic messages API references set 1.0. All read
July 2026. Recommendations outside this range exist: Mistral's instruct model cards suggest
0.15.} and every engine implements truncation samplers beside them. Truncation samplers,
among them top-$p$, min-$p$, and top-$n\sigma$, discard the tail of the distribution before the
draw, each by its own criterion. They were designed to keep open-ended text coherent at high
temperature \citep{garcesarias2025decoding}, and they are now also evaluated on tasks with one
checkable answer, where the claim is accuracy: min-$p$ reports gains on GSM8K
\citep{nguyen2024minp}, and top-$n\sigma$ reports accuracy held up to a temperature of 3.0
\citep{tang2024topn}.

Both results rest on one model and one range of temperatures. Top-$n\sigma$ was evaluated on
Llama-3-8B \citep{tang2024topn} and min-$p$ on Mistral-7B \citep{nguyen2024minp}, at
temperatures of 1.5 to 3, above the defaults systems set. A reanalysis that searched the same
number of hyperparameter settings for every rule found the gains of min-$p$ indistinguishable
from those of the other rules \citep{schaeffer2025minp}. The question cannot be settled by
comparing across papers, because benchmark scores move with the inference backend even under
greedy decoding \citep{masoudian2026backend}, and with the prompt, the parser, and the item set.
Settling it requires one pipeline in which everything except the decoding configuration is held
fixed. It also requires enough models to show whether the answer depends on the model.

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Research question:} Under what model and temperature conditions do truncation samplers
improve accuracy over plain temperature sampling?
\end{quote}

We test thirteen open-weight models from the Llama-3, Mistral, Qwen, Gemma, and OLMo families
on GSM8K and MMLU-Pro at temperatures 0.7, 1.0, and 1.3, in one pipeline with fixed items,
prompts, token budgets, and parser. Ten of the models also run under eight decoding
configurations: greedy decoding, plain temperature sampling, and six truncation configurations.
Section~\ref{sec:design} gives the design. Two questions follow. The first is whether a model
keeps its accuracy between 0.7 and 1.3 under plain sampling, and what the lost accuracy looks
like when it does not. The second is whether any truncation rule improves on plain temperature
sampling between 0.7 and 1.3.

We find that temperature sensitivity differs by model. Between 0.7 and 1.3, six of the thirteen
models lose 17 to 38 accuracy points on MMLU-Pro under plain sampling, and the other seven lose
at most 10. The loss takes the form of a collapse of the output. On the six fragile models, the
share of generations that run to the token limit or never state an answer rises by 26 to 78
points, while the share of generations that state a wrong answer does not rise. The collapse is
a property of the model. Hermes-3, a different post-training of the same base model as
Llama-3.1-8B, loses half as much accuracy as Llama-3.1-8B does. Truncation gains follow the
collapse. On the models that hold their accuracy, no truncation sampler improves on plain
temperature sampling at 0.7 or 1.0. On the models that collapse, every truncation sampler
improves accuracy at 1.3, and at temperatures up to 2.0 truncation delays or prevents the
collapse. The gains reported for truncation samplers are therefore real, and they are recoveries
from a collapse that only some models suffer and that begins above the temperatures systems
use. This work makes three contributions:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{A controlled comparison of truncation samplers at the temperatures systems use.}
Across thirteen open-weight models on GSM8K and MMLU-Pro, no truncation sampler improves on
plain temperature sampling at 0.7 or 1.0, and an upper bound states how large a gain the
results leave possible.
\item \textbf{Temperature fragility as a property of the model.} Six of the thirteen models
collapse between 1.0 and 1.3 into generations that run to the token limit or never state an
answer. The collapse replicates across inference engine and precision. A different
post-training of the same base model halves it. A plain temperature test identifies the
fragile models before any sampler is compared.
\item \textbf{The condition under which truncation samplers help.} On the collapsing models,
every truncation sampler improves accuracy at 1.3 and delays the collapse at temperatures up
to 2.0. This locates the gains reported for truncation samplers above the temperatures systems
use, which reconciles those reports with the absence of gains below.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:background}

\textbf{Truncation samplers.} A truncation sampler removes the least probable tokens from the
distribution before each token is sampled. Top-$k$ keeps the $k$ most probable tokens
\citep{fan2018hierarchical}. Top-$p$, or nucleus sampling, keeps the smallest set of tokens
whose probabilities sum to $p$, so the set grows when the distribution is flat
\citep{holtzman2020curious}. Min-$p$ keeps every token whose probability is at least a fixed
fraction of the largest \citep{nguyen2024minp}. Top-$n\sigma$ works on the logits, the model's
scores before they are converted to probabilities, and keeps the tokens within $n$ standard
deviations of the largest logit \citep{tang2024topn}. Temperature rescales all logits equally,
so top-$n\sigma$ keeps the same tokens at every temperature, and so does top-$k$, because
scaling does not change the order of the tokens. Top-$p$ and min-$p$ decide from the
probabilities themselves, which temperature changes, so for them the order of scaling and
truncation matters. A sampler applied after temperature scaling sees the scaled distribution
and keeps a different set of tokens from the same sampler applied before it. Our main
configurations apply temperature first, and for top-$p$ and min-$p$ we also run the reverse
order, which is the default in llama.cpp.

\textbf{Temperature-invariant samplers.} The parameter of top-$p$ or min-$p$ fixes how much of
the distribution is kept, and temperature reshapes the distribution. The same value therefore
keeps a different set of tokens at each temperature, and a value that works well at one
temperature need not work well at another. Two samplers published in 2026 begin from this
observation. $p$-less sampling \citep{tan2026pless} and Min-$k$ \citep{ding2026mink} each
decide which tokens to keep from the shape of the distribution at every step, so that
temperature does not change which tokens are kept. Neither paper measures whether that matters
at the temperatures systems use. The evaluation of Min-$k$ begins at temperature 1.0, where
top-$k$, top-$p$, and min-$p$ score within a point of one another on GSM8K, and the samplers
separate only above temperature 2 \citep{ding2026mink}.

\textbf{Evaluations of decoding and temperature.} Studies that compare decoding methods find
that the best one depends on the model. A survey of decoding methods reports that the best
method changes with the task and the model \citep{shi2024decoding}. The best configuration for
open-ended generation varies by model \citep{garcesarias2025decoding}, and so does the gap
between greedy decoding and sampling on reasoning benchmarks \citep{song2024goodbad}. Studies
of temperature either stop at 1.0 or use tasks with little accuracy to lose.
\citet{renze2024temperature} find no effect of temperature between 0.0 and 1.0 on
multiple-choice problems. \citet{grover2026reliability} concludes that instruction tuning is
the main determinant of robustness to temperature, on synthetic tasks where the most robust
models score 11 to 30\% and with no truncation sampler tested. \citet{fastowski2025confidence}
link the entropy of the token distribution to temperature sensitivity on factual questions,
and \citet{troshin2025temperature} attribute high-temperature losses on mathematics to wrong
tokens at a few critical positions.

\textbf{Conditions of measurement.} One recent study shapes the design of our comparison.
\citet{masoudian2026backend} run three instruction-tuned models through five inference
frameworks on six benchmarks and find that the framework changes the scores even under greedy
decoding, by amounts that depend on the model. A comparison across models and samplers must
therefore hold the framework fixed, or the differences it reports may belong to the framework.
We run every configuration through one engine, llama.cpp \citep{gerganov2023llamacpp}. To check whether the collapse we
observe comes from the engine, we rerun the most fragile model through a second engine at full
precision. Our models also run quantized, with weights stored at reduced precision to fit in less memory.
\citet{prasad2026quantization} find that quantization
does not change how much a model loses to temperature, and our grid at four quantization
levels agrees (Appendix~\ref{app:temp}).

\label{sec:design}

differs between them, up to the sampling variation that the confidence intervals quantify.

\textbf{Decoding.} Every model in the main grid runs under eight decoding configurations. Two
are baselines, greedy decoding and plain temperature sampling with no truncation. Four apply
one truncation sampler after temperature scaling, each at the setting its source paper uses:
top-$p$ 0.95 \citep{holtzman2020curious}, top-$k$ 40 \citep{radford2019gpt2}, min-$p$ 0.05
\citep{nguyen2024minp}, and top-$n\sigma$ 1.0 \citep{tang2024topn}. The last two repeat top-$p$
and min-$p$ with truncation applied before temperature scaling, the default order in llama.cpp,
so both orders are tested for the two samplers where the order matters
(Section~\ref{sec:background}). Temperatures 0.7 and 1.0 bracket the defaults of
Section~\ref{sec:intro}, and 1.3 sits above them, to show what happens when a model is run past
its default range.

\textbf{Tasks.} GSM8K is a set of grade-school word problems with a numeric answer
\citep{cobbe2021gsm8k}. We use the zero-shot chain-of-thought prompt of Meta's Llama evaluation
suite, which asks for reasoning without worked examples \citep{meta2024llamaevals}. MMLU-Pro is
ten-option multiple choice across 14 categories \citep{wang2024mmlupro}, prompted the same way.
Both tasks need a chain of reasoning that ends in one answer that can be checked exactly, and
MMLU-Pro's chains run about twice as long (median 398 against 212 completion tokens at
temperature 0.7). Each task uses 50 fixed items, the first 50 of its test split.

\textbf{Grading.} Each generation has a token budget, 512 tokens on GSM8K and 1024 on MMLU-Pro.
A generation that is still running when it reaches the budget is cut off there, and we call it
\emph{capped}. The parsers follow lm-eval-harness \citep{gao2023evalharness}, the framework
behind most published scores on these tasks, so our grading matches the scores readers know and
no parsing choice of ours can favour one configuration. The parser records one of three
outcomes. A generation is \emph{strict} when it contains the final-answer sentence the prompt
asks for (``The final answer is 26''; ``The answer is (I)''). It is \emph{flexible} when that
sentence is absent and the parser falls back to the last number or the last parenthesized
letter. It is \emph{failed} when neither exists, and it is then graded incorrect.
Appendix~\ref{app:grading} shows one generation of each kind.

\textbf{Models.} The main grid holds seven instruction-tuned models from five families:
Llama-3.1-8B \citep{grattafiori2024llama}, Mistral-7B-v0.3 \citep{jiang2023mistral}, Qwen2.5-7B
\citep{yang2024qwen25}, Qwen3-1.7B, 4B, and 8B with thinking off \citep{yang2025qwen3}, and
Gemma-3-12B \citep{gemma2025gemma3}. They are the widely used open-weight families at sizes that
fit the single 16\,GB consumer GPU on which every run was made (Appendix~\ref{app:repro}). Each
runs at four quantization levels, Q8\_0, Q6\_K, Q4\_K\_M, and Q3\_K\_M, where the digit is the
number of bits per weight, all produced by one provider with the same conversion settings
(Appendix~\ref{app:repro}), so the levels differ only in precision. Every temperature result in
the paper uses Q8\_0, the level closest to full precision. Six more models run the temperature
test at Q8\_0. Two are further Llama-3 releases, Llama-3-8B \citep{meta2024llama3} and
Llama-3.2-3B \citep{meta2024llama32}, added because Llama-3.1-8B collapsed in the grid and we
wanted to know whether the other releases of the family do. Hermes-3-8B \citep{teknium2024hermes3}
is a different post-training of the Llama-3.1-8B base, which separates the base model from its
post-training. Qwen3.5-9B \citep{qwen2026qwen35} and Gemma-4-E4B \citep{google2026gemma4} are
two releases from 2026, added to test whether current models show the same pattern, and
OLMo-3-7B \citep{ai2025olmo3} is a fully open model. Appendix~\ref{app:repro} gives the model
files, their hashes, and the llama.cpp builds.

\textbf{Fragility flag.} The six additional models first run the temperature test alone. A
model then receives the eight decoding configurations only if the test shows that it loses
accuracy to temperature, because the sampler comparison on models that hold their accuracy is
already covered by the main grid. We fixed the criterion before any of the six ran. A model
qualifies when, between temperatures 0.7 and 1.3 under plain sampling, its accuracy falls by at
least 15 points, its capped rate rises by at least 20 points, or its share of strict parses
falls by at least 20 points, on either task. Three models qualified, Hermes-3, Qwen3.5-9B, and
OLMo-3-7B, and each ran the eight configurations at Q8\_0.

\textbf{Engine check.} Every result in the grid comes from llama.cpp on quantized weights, so a
collapse could come from the engine or from the conversion of the weights. To check, we rerun
the temperature test through Hugging Face Transformers \citep{wolf2020transformers} on the
official weights: Llama-3.2-3B at full 16-bit precision, Llama-3.1-8B in 8-bit weights, the
largest that fit the GPU, and Qwen3-4B at full precision as a model that holds its accuracy.

\textbf{Statistical inference.} All intervals in the paper are 95\% confidence intervals
obtained by bootstrapping over items. We resample the 50 items in each task, keeping all
generations of an item together. This captures uncertainty due to the choice of items without
treating repeated generations as independent observations. Comparisons between configurations
are paired because each configuration is evaluated on the same 50 items. In
Section~\ref{sec:bound}, we ask whether any truncation sampler improves on plain temperature
sampling. To account for testing several samplers, we resample their differences from
temperature sampling jointly and compute an upper confidence bound on the largest improvement
\citep{westfall1993resampling}.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Temperature sensitivity differs across checkpoints}
\label{sec:sensitivity}

Figure~\ref{fig:drops} gives the plain-temperature accuracy drop from 0.7 to 1.3 for all
thirteen models at Q8\_0, and Appendix~\ref{app:temp} the accuracies at every temperature.
Six models lose 17 to 38 points on MMLU-Pro: Llama-3.1-8B, Llama-3.2-3B, Qwen3.5-9B,
Hermes-3-8B, OLMo-3-7B, and Llama-3-8B. The sensitivity is graded within the six. Three have
interval lower limits at or above 15 (Llama-3.1, Llama-3.2, Qwen3.5); the other three trip the
flag on point estimates, with intervals that overlap Mistral's. The other seven lose at most 10,
with intervals that all include 4 or less; we call these seven robust, meaning unflagged in the
tested range. On GSM8K, the shorter task, Llama-3.1 and Llama-3.2 lose 30 and 38 points as well, and the
other four fragile models lose 4 to 12; for those four the flag fires on MMLU-Pro only. Llama-3.1's decline has begun by temperature 1.0, where its MMLU-Pro
accuracy is already 10 points below its 0.7 level. Accuracy at 0.7 does not predict the drop: Mistral-7B at 31\% on GSM8K and Qwen3-4B at 96\% both
hold, while Llama-3.1 at 77\% collapses. Model age does not predict it either, since
Mistral-7B-v0.3 predates Llama-3.1.

The collapse is a property of the checkpoint under fixed conditions, and it survives a change of
engine. Through Hugging Face Transformers on its official BF16 weights, Llama-3.2-3B loses 40.0 points on
GSM8K and 36.0 on MMLU-Pro (Q8\_0 through llama.cpp: 38.0 and 36.7), with 61 and 92\% of its
generations capped at 1.3; the paired differences between the two engines' drops are $+2.0$
[$-8.7$, 12.0] and $-0.7$ [$-9.3$, 8.7]. Llama-3.1-8B in 8-bit weights loses 43.3 and 42.7, and
Qwen3-4B in BF16 moves by 1.3 and $-0.7$, as in its GGUF cells. These runs replicate the collapse. They do not show that the engines are equivalent: the
intervals on the differences span about 20 points, and Llama-3.1's GSM8K drop is 13.3 points
larger in 8-bit than at Q8\_0 [2.7, 23.3] (Appendix~\ref{app:repro}). Llama-3.2-3B and Qwen3-4B rerun on the later llama.cpp release reproduce their pinned-build
drops with differences that include zero, and the stratified MMLU-Pro subset preserves Llama's
much larger drop (Llama-3.1 $+43.3$ [31.3, 55.3]; Qwen2.5 $+6.0$; Gemma-3 $+7.3$).

Hermes-3-8B shares the Llama-3.1-8B base and shows roughly half of its MMLU-Pro drop (19.3
against 38.0), with the same rise in capped generations. The two checkpoints differ only in
post-training, so post-training changed the size of the collapse; which part of it did, the
design cannot say. The six fragile
checkpoints span three lineages, Llama-derived, Qwen, and OLMo, but the three Meta releases
share data and methods, so they are not three independent observations.

\subsection{The loss is capped or unparseable generations}
\label{sec:degeneration}

This subsection uses plain temperature sampling throughout. An accuracy drop can come from two
failures, completed answers that are wrong or generations that never reach an answer, and the
parser fields separate them. Figure~\ref{fig:outcomes} splits every MMLU-Pro generation into
three exclusive outcomes: correct; capped or unparseable (the generation reached its token
budget, or contained no answer to grade); and answered wrong (a parsed answer that is wrong
and ended before the budget). In all six fragile models the capped-or-unparseable share rises
between 0.7 and 1.3, by 26 to 78 points, and the answered-wrong share falls or stays flat
(Llama-3.1: 31 to 3; Hermes-3: 49 to 29; Qwen3.5: 9 to 9). In the seven robust models the
capped share moves by at most 11 points. GSM8K shows the same pattern for the two Llama
checkpoints (Appendix~\ref{app:grading}).

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{fig2_outcomes}
\caption{Outcome of every MMLU-Pro generation under plain temperature sampling at Q8\_0, three
bars per model, top to bottom $T{=}0.7$, 1.0, and 1.3. Categories are exclusive, in this
precedence: correct; capped or unparseable (so this category holds incorrect generations only);
answered wrong. Model order as in Figure~\ref{fig:drops}.}
\label{fig:outcomes}

What the capped generations contain differs by temperature. A blinded audit of 152 sampled
outputs, labelled by a language-model annotator that saw only the task and the raw text,
found all eight audited Llama-3.1 generations capped at 1.3 to be incoherent token noise, all
eight capped at 0.7 to be coherent reasoning cut off by the budget, and 15 of the 26 parse failures
to contain a clear answer in an unexpected form; 34 of the 152 labels were marked borderline, and the audit covers
four grid models and none of the flagged panel models (Appendix~\ref{app:grading}). Budget
censoring is visible at 0.7 in the verbose models: OLMo-3 and Qwen3.5 already cap 47 and 29\% of
their MMLU-Pro generations there. The audit establishes degeneration in the sampled Llama
outputs at 1.3; the category as a whole also contains budget and formatting failures. Qwen3.5 is unaudited, and its parser fields show a rise of only 13 points in the
capped rate against a fall of 31 in the strict-parse rate, so most of its generations that
reach no answer end before the budget.

Among the generations that do complete with the pinned final-answer sentence, accuracy at 1.3 is
subject to selection, because the surviving generations can come from a different mix of items.
The matched comparison is greedy accuracy on the surviving generations' items, each survivor
counted once. On MMLU-Pro,
Llama-3.1's completed answers score 78.9\% against 78.9\% for matched greedy, Qwen3.5's 83.1
against 84.7, and OLMo-3's 73.3 against 73.3; for Llama-3-8B, Llama-3.2-3B (nine survivors), and
Hermes-3 the completed answers score below matched greedy, by 4, 11, and 16 points
(Appendix~\ref{app:grading}). Three fragile checkpoints score 4 to 16 points below matched greedy among their surviving
answers, in point estimates, and so does robust Mistral-7B (21 points), so a survivor gap at 1.3
is not specific to fragility (Table~\ref{tab:survivor} gives the 0.7 reference for every
model).

\subsection{Measured sampler gains are small where plain temperature holds}
\label{sec:bound}

Sampler gains are small on the six robust grid models at temperatures 0.7 and 1.0. For each
model, task, and temperature we compare each truncation rule with plain temperature on the same
items (all contrasts in Appendix~\ref{app:sampler}), and Table~\ref{tab:bound} states the
family of comparisons behind each claim. On the six grid models that hold their accuracy, at temperatures 0.7 and 1.0 and
pooled over quantization, the largest gain over 96 comparisons is 3.3 points, 91 of the 96 point estimates lie within 3 points of
zero, 1 interval lies above zero against 3 below, and the simultaneous 95\% upper bound on the
largest gain is 9.0. Restricted to Q8\_0, the same 96 contrasts have a largest gain of 4.7, no
interval above zero, and a bound of 16.0.

On the same six models at 1.3, gains reach 9 points and 13 of 48 intervals lie above zero, on
Mistral, Qwen2.5, and Gemma-3, the three with the largest drops among the robust seven.
Llama-3.1 is different already at 1.0: on MMLU-Pro, where its plain-temperature accuracy has
fallen 10 points, min-$p$ and top-$n\sigma$ gain 11 points (pooled), and on the flagged models'
Q8\_0 grids Hermes-3 gains 8 with top-$n\sigma$ at the same temperature.

The two ablations are outside these families. Applying top-$p$ or min-$p$ before temperature
scaling changes accuracy by at most 4 points on the seven main-grid models at $T \le 1.0$. The
largest gain over plain temperature anywhere at $T \le 1.0$ is the temperature-last top-$p$ on
Hermes-3 at 0.7 on MMLU-Pro, $+12.0$ [4.7, 19.3], one contrast among 48 ablation contrasts on
the flagged models that otherwise stay within 7 points. On Llama-3.1 at 1.3, truncating first
keeps 22 more points under top-$p$ on MMLU-Pro: at that temperature the rule cuts a sharper
distribution and removes more of the tail (Appendix~\ref{app:sampler}).

\subsection{Truncation recovers accuracy as plain temperature loses it}
\label{sec:recovery}

Across the 60 model, task, and temperature combinations with a sampler grid (seven grid models
at Q8\_0 and three flagged models), larger losses of plain temperature against greedy decoding accompany
larger gains of truncation over plain temperature (Pearson $r{=}0.89$ over the 60 best-rule
points; 0.78 over the 240 rule-level points). The association is descriptive, and the two
quantities share the plain-temperature baseline (Appendix~\ref{app:sampler}).
Figure~\ref{fig:recovery} shows the recovery contrasts for the four fragile checkpoints with a
grid at temperature 1.3. For the flagged models these contrasts use the plain-temperature
baselines rerun within the sampler grid, which are a second run of the same cells with different seeds and differ from the extension
run's by up to 6 points (Hermes-3's GSM8K accuracy at 0.7 is 77.3 there and 71.3 here; Qwen3.5's
MMLU-Pro accuracy at 1.3 is 32.7 and 38.0). Every rule's point estimate is positive on every model and task, and 20 of the 24 intervals on
the flagged models exclude zero. The right panel compares each rule with plain temperature at 0.7. In all eight model-task
cells the best rule finishes within six points of that level, in point estimates: top-$n\sigma$ returns
Llama-3.1's MMLU-Pro accuracy from 10.0 to 52.0\% and Qwen3.5's from 38.0 to 59.3\%, and min-$p$
returns Hermes-3's from 26.0 to 46.0\% and OLMo-3's from 26.7 to 42.7\%. The recovery is not
uniform: top-$k$ leaves Hermes-3 8 points below its 0.7 level on MMLU-Pro, min-$p$ leaves OLMo-3
11 points below on GSM8K, and top-$p$ leaves Llama-3.1 31 points below on MMLU-Pro. Every rule
was compared with plain temperature only, and we do not rank the rules.


\textbf{Where each model collapses.} To see whether the robust models also collapse, the ladder
run extends the temperature to 2.0 on Llama-3.1-8B, Qwen2.5-7B, and Gemma-3-12B under four
configurations (Figure~\ref{fig:ladder}; drops with intervals in Appendix~\ref{app:ladder}).
Call a configuration collapsed at the lowest tested temperature at which its accuracy falls
below half of the same model and task's plain-temperature accuracy at 0.7 (point estimates on
the tested grid). Under plain temperature the three models collapse on GSM8K at 1.5, 1.7, and
2.0, in the order of Figure~\ref{fig:drops}, and at 1.5 Llama's drop interval is disjoint from
the other two models'. On MMLU-Pro, the longer task, the crossings come at 1.3, 1.5, and 1.5.
Truncation delays or avoids the crossing: top-$p$ delays it in three of the six model-task
combinations and leaves it unchanged in the other three; min-$p$ delays it to 2.0 on Llama's
GSM8K and 1.7 on Llama's MMLU-Pro and avoids it in the tested range on the other two models; top-$n\sigma$ never crosses it on any
model or task through 2.0. Min-$p$ and top-$n\sigma$ were validated at temperatures of 1.5 to 3
\citep{nguyen2024minp,tang2024topn}, a range that overlaps the collapse temperatures we observe,
and that is where their gains are largest here too.


\section{Discussion and conclusion}
\label{sec:discussion}

Under plain temperature sampling, some checkpoints keep their accuracy above the default
temperatures and others lose it by 1.3, and the loss appears as capped or unparseable
generations. Larger plain-temperature losses accompany larger truncation gains; the recovery
contrasts of Figure~\ref{fig:recovery} carry that claim on their own, and the association across
all cells is descriptive. This reconciles the sampler literature with the small gains we measure
at default temperatures. Min-$p$ and top-$n\sigma$ were evaluated in a range that overlaps the
collapse temperatures we observe, and there they help; Min-$k$'s comparison at temperature 1.0
shows the probability-based rules within a point of each other \citep{ding2026mink}.

For a practitioner running objective tasks, the two settings should be chosen together. On a
model that holds its accuracy at the intended temperature, the gain from a standard truncation
rule is small: across 96 contrasts on the six robust grid models at 0.7 and 1.0, the largest
estimated gain is 3.3 points and the simultaneous upper bound 9.0 (16.0 at Q8\_0 alone). On a model that loses
accuracy there, either a lower temperature or a truncation rule recovers most of the loss, and
the fragility can be measured without gold answers: the capped rate and the strict-parse rate
need no labels, and their change between 0.7 and 1.3 separates the fragile models from the
robust ones in every run of this study, including the panel run after the flag was written. We measure accuracy
only, so we say nothing about the output diversity a higher temperature buys.

For sampler evaluations, a gain should be reported next to the plain-temperature loss it
recovers, since a gain measured beyond one model's collapse temperature can be absent on a model that
collapses later. What moves the collapse temperature remains open. Hermes-3 shows
that post-training changes its size on one base, and the grid contains no base-versus-instruct
or checkpoint axis, so the cause is not identified here.

\section*{Limitations}

Each task has 50 fixed items with $K{=}3$ repetitions; the small item count is what makes a
factorial run of 185,600 generations feasible, and every main estimate carries an item-clustered
interval. The fixed MMLU-Pro subset is single-category (business) because the test split is
sorted by category; the stratified subset preserves Llama's much larger drop on 50 fresh items, and as a second
sample of 50 it does not estimate the benchmark-wide effect either. Mistral-7B scores 31\% on
GSM8K and 29\% on MMLU-Pro at 0.7, which limits the power to detect a further drop on a model we
count as robust.

Temperature 1.3 is a stress point above the defaults, chosen to bound the deployment range from
above. The token budgets are fixed, and budget censoring is visible at 0.7 on the verbose
models; the audit distinguishes cut-off reasoning from degenerate text on a sample of 152
outputs from four grid models, labelled by a language model with 34 borderline cases; the
remaining records, including every output of the three flagged panel models, are unaudited. The
survivor comparison controls for item composition with matched greedy decoding; it does not
remove the selection.

The sampler evidence is narrower than the temperature evidence. Thirteen models ran the
temperature test; ten ran a sampler grid, and the three flagged models were selected by their
temperature drop, so the recovery estimates describe a fragility-defined subgroup. ``Best rule''
is chosen after the fact, which favours the recovery. Rules were tested at one setting each,
in one engine, and the bound applies to those settings. The two engine checks replicate the
collapse for two Llama checkpoints and one control; Llama-3.1's check ran in 8-bit weights, its
native precision untested, and the intervals on the between-engine differences are about 20 points wide.
Repeated generations, temperatures, and rules are not independent observations, so the
gain-versus-loss correlation is descriptive.

The scope is open-weight instruction-tuned models of 1.7B to 12B parameters, in English,
zero-shot, on objective tasks with one checkable answer, with thinking mode off. A spot-check
of Qwen3-8B with thinking on shows a 10-point MMLU-Pro drop [2, 20] at $N{=}25$
(Appendix~\ref{app:temp}), so the bound does not cover thinking mode. Open-ended generation, the
original use of truncation samplers, lies outside the design, as does the diversity a higher
temperature buys under multi-sample selection. The study identifies no cause for the
differences between checkpoints.

\section{Reproducibility and engine checks}
\label{app:repro}

\textbf{Inference stack.} llama.cpp at commit \texttt{5aba5364} (server build 9456), native
\texttt{/completion} endpoint, CUDA builds per GPU architecture (sm\_75 for an RTX 2080~Ti,
sm\_120 for an RTX 5070~Ti). Server-side BOS insertion is disabled; the client-rendered chat
template owns the special tokens, and each record stores the SHA-256 of the rendered prompt.
The 2026 panel, its sampler grids, and the bridge run use release v0.4.0 (commit
\texttt{5266f24d}), which two panel models require, with the same client-side rendering and the
server's template parser off. That release forces its own BOS for Gemma~4, so the Gemma-4 prompt
is rendered without one; on both builds the server returns some cap-length degenerate streams with a zero token count,
the pinned build with their text (which the client recovers, and which the audit labelled) and
v0.4.0 as an HTTP error without text. The runner records those as zero-token capped generations.
They number 189 of 185,600 records in the main grid, 114 in the Llama-releases run, 158 in the
panel, 196 in its sampler grids, 64 in the engine check, 73 in the bridge, and 2,549 on the
ladder; at $T{=}1.3$ under plain temperature they are 13\% of Llama-3.1's capped generations. Every quantized checkpoint is a Bartowski imatrix
GGUF; SHA-256 hashes of the model files are in the repository (\texttt{DOWNLOADS.md}).

\textbf{Decoding and seeding.} Exactly one truncation parameter per request; the chain order is
pinned through the server's \texttt{samplers} array and recorded in every record. At both builds
the server instantiates only the samplers named in that array, so its defaults for unlisted
samplers (top-$k$ 40, top-$p$ 0.95, min-$p$ 0.05) are inert, and no penalty, DRY, XTC, or
logit-bias parameter is sent. Every generation's seed is derived from (global seed 0, model,
quantization, sampler, temperature, task, item, repetition). Batched serving ($\le 8$ slots) is
seed-logged but not bit-exact. Tasks: GSM8K (openai/gsm8k, test split, first 50 items) and
MMLU-Pro (TIGER-Lab/MMLU-Pro, test split, first 50 items). Each generation is one JSONL record
with the identity tuple, request parameters, raw output, parse outcome, and token counts; runs
are resumable by record id. The pipeline, configurations, analysis scripts, and every record are
at \url{https://anonymous.4open.science/r/decoding-robustness-027C}.

\textbf{Engine check.} The transformers configuration loads \texttt{meta-llama/Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct}
(revision \texttt{0cb88a4f}) in BF16 with transformers 5.16.1 and torch 2.14, samples with
\texttt{do\_sample} at temperature $T$ with top-$k$ and top-$p$ disabled and no other processor,
in batches of eight with left padding; greedy decoding is \texttt{do\_sample=False}. Generation
stops on the model's end-of-sequence set or at the task's budget. The prompt is rendered from
the same template as the grid; the template stamps the run date into the Llama system header,
the only difference between the runs' prompt strings, and the prompt token counts agree on every
item. Llama-3.1-8B loads the \texttt{unsloth/Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct} mirror of the official
