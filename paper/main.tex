\begin{abstract}
Truncation samplers such as top-$p$, min-$p$, and top-$n\sigma$ exist to prevent the
accuracy collapse of high-temperature sampling, and their evidence comes from
temperatures where every model has already collapsed. We ask how instruction-tuned
models behave at the temperatures deployments use, from the default temperatures of 0.6 to
1.0 up to a test temperature of $T{=}1.3$ above that range, and when a truncation rule changes the outcome. In
one controlled pipeline we run nine models from five families across eight decoding
configurations, four quantization levels, and two tasks with a checkable answer, GSM8K and
MMLU-Pro. Temperature sensitivity varies sharply by model. Under plain temperature
sampling at $T{=}1.3$, Llama-3.1-8B and Llama-3.2-3B lose 30 to 38 accuracy points and
Llama-3-8B loses 17 on the longer task, while no model outside the family loses more
than 10. The loss is degeneration. Generations become incoherent, run on until the
maximum token count, and never reach an answer, while the well-formed answers stay as
accurate as under greedy decoding. The collapse replicates on both Llama checkpoints through a second inference engine, at native precision for Llama-3.2-3B, so
neither the engine nor the quantization causes it. At the default temperatures no
truncation rule gains more than 3 points on any robust model (8 at the 95\%
upper limit); gains of 3 to 10 points appear only at $T{=}1.3$ on the models nearest
their own collapse temperature, and tests up to $T{=}2.0$ show each model collapsing at
a different temperature, with truncation improving accuracy only above it.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Sampling is the default decoding choice for deployed language models
\citep{shi2024decoding}, and truncation samplers exist to prevent accuracy loss at high
temperature. They were designed for open-ended generation, where output variety is the
goal \citep{zhou2024decoding}, but they are now also evaluated on tasks with one
checkable answer. Min-$p$ reports accuracy gains over top-$p$ on GSM8K at high
temperature \citep{nguyen2024minp}, and top-$n\sigma$ reports accuracy maintained up to
$T{=}3.0$ \citep{tang2024topn}. Both results assume that raising the temperature eventually reduces every model's accuracy and a good sampler reduces that
loss. We test that premise at the temperatures deployments actually use and ask
\textbf{which models collapse in that range, and whether the sampler changes anything
before they do}.

There is a good reason to expect the collapse. At each step the decoder draws the next
token at random from the model's predicted distribution, and a temperature parameter scales the randomness of the draw (greedy decoding instead always takes the most probable token). Raising it
moves probability from the model's preferred tokens toward the tail of the distribution,
the tokens with low predicted probability, where the model's estimates are least reliable, so a
high-temperature draw can select a token that fits the context poorly
\citep{holtzman2020curious,hewitt2022truncation}. The selected token then becomes
context for every later step, and the continuation can degenerate into incoherent text.
Truncation samplers prevent this degeneration by deleting the tail of the distribution
before the draw, and the rules differ in which tokens they exclude.

The accuracy evidence has two problems. First, it generalizes from very few models.
Top-$n\sigma$'s results come from a single model, Llama-3-8B \citep{tang2024topn}, and
min-$p$'s main results from Mistral-7B \citep{nguyen2024minp}. Second, the gains are
demonstrated at temperatures of 1.5 to 3, while deployed systems set defaults between
0.6 and 1.0 (Section~\ref{sec:setup}). 

Across nine instruction-tuned models from five families, run through one controlled
pipeline, temperature sensitivity inside that range turns out to be a property of the
model. Six models lose no more than 10 points at $T{\le}1.3$ under every sampler we
test. Llama-3.1-8B and Llama-3.2-3B lose 30 to 38 points under plain temperature
sampling, and Llama-3-8B, top-$n\sigma$'s test model, loses 17 on the longer task.

Our contributions:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Heterogeneous temperature sensitivity, measured in one pipeline.} Seven
    instruction-tuned models, four GGUF quantization levels, eight decoding configurations, and
    $T \in \{0.7, 1.0, 1.3\}$ on GSM8K and MMLU-Pro ($N{=}50$ items, $K{=}3$
    repetitions), 185{,}600 generations, plus the same temperature tests on two further
    Llama releases, with item-clustered bootstrap CIs on every main estimate. Every model that loses more than 10 points is a Llama-3 release
    (Section~\ref{sec:modelbound}).
  \item \textbf{A replicated failure pattern.} During the collapse, generations become
    incoherent and run on until the token limit. Among Llama's generations that reach a well-formed answer at $T{=}1.3$,
    accuracy matches greedy decoding on the same items, so the lost generations are
    the ones that never reach an answer (Section~\ref{sec:degeneration}). The same
    failure occurs under a second engine and at native precision: through HF transformers,
    Llama-3.2-3B collapses the same way in native BF16 and Llama-3.1-8B in 8-bit, while
    Qwen3-4B stays flat (Section~\ref{sec:replication}).
  \item \textbf{A conditional sampler benefit.} At the default temperatures no truncation rule
    gains more than 3 points on any robust model (8 at the 95\% upper limit). Gains
    appear only as a model approaches its cliff, the lowest temperature at which it
    loses more than half its accuracy, and tests up to $T{=}2.0$ put the three cliffs we
    measure at different temperatures, Llama's inside the deployment range
    (Section~\ref{sec:spread}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Truncation samplers.}
Top-$k$ sampling draws from only the $k$ most probable tokens; it was introduced for
story generation, where likelihood-maximizing decoding produced generic and repetitive
text \citep{fan2018hierarchical}. \citet{holtzman2020curious} showed that a fixed $k$
fits poorly because the next-token distribution is sometimes peaked and sometimes flat,
and proposed nucleus sampling, which keeps the smallest set of tokens whose probabilities
sum to a target $p$. Min-$p$ keeps only the tokens whose probability is at least a set fraction of the highest-probability token's, so the rule excludes more tokens when that probability is high and fewer when it is low \citep{nguyen2024minp}.
Top-$n\sigma$ selects tokens using logits, the scores the model assigns before converting them to probabilities, keeping tokens within $n$ standard deviations of the top logit; temperature rescales all logits equally, so the retained set does not change with temperature \citep{tang2024topn}. \citet{welleck2020consistency} proved that top-$k$ and nucleus decoding can produce
sequences that never terminate even when the model itself always terminates, so
non-termination is a documented decoding failure. In contrast to the sampler papers, which
compare truncation rules against each other, we test whether the failure they all aim to
prevent occurs at deployment temperatures at all.

\paragraph{Reproducibility of sampler gains.}
\citet{schaeffer2025minp} reanalyzed min-$p$ on nine models and 31 temperatures, giving
every sampler an equal number of hyperparameter settings, and found its reported gains
``largely indistinguishable'' from the other samplers'. A second variable in such a
comparison is the order in which temperature and truncation are applied. The order matters because the
truncation threshold is computed on whichever distribution the rule sees: applied before
temperature, the rule cuts the model's original, sharp distribution; applied after, it
cuts one that temperature has already flattened, so the same nominal setting keeps a
different set of tokens. We run the two chain orders as explicit configurations
(Section~\ref{sec:setup}). \citet{zhou2024decoding} tests a range of decoding parameter values on open-ended generation with
quality and diversity metrics, while our tasks have one checkable answer.
\citet{shi2024decoding} compare decoding methods on closed-ended tasks and report that
the best method changes with the task and the model, and that quantization alters how
robust a model is to the choice; the comparison is again among methods, and it does not establish whether models lose accuracy at deployment temperatures.
\citet{song2024goodbad} documents per-model gaps between greedy decoding and sampling on
reasoning benchmarks and treats the gaps as evaluation noise to report; we study the difference and find that it results from a termination failure in one model family.

\paragraph{Robustness to decoding randomness.}
\citet{renze2024temperature} sampled temperatures from 0.0 to 1.6 on multiple-choice
problem solving and found no statistically significant effect on accuracy between 0.0 and
1.0. We also find no statistically significant effect for most models, but per-model results show Llama-3.1-8B losing accuracy just above $T{=}1.0$ while the other models remain comparatively stable (Section~\ref{sec:modelbound}).
The nearest study is \citet{grover2026reliability}, which also asks what makes a model
robust to decoding randomness and concludes that instruction tuning is the primary
determinant. Our data refine that conclusion. All nine of our models are instruction-tuned and one family still collapses, so instruction tuning alone does not explain the differences. Their evaluation differs from ours in two respects that matter. They test temperatures only up to $T{=}1.0$, and the Llama collapse happens between 1.0 and 1.3; and their robust
models score 11 to 30\% on synthetic tasks, so there is little accuracy left to lose.
They also test no truncation samplers.
\citet{fastowski2025confidence} connect token-distribution entropy to temperature
sensitivity on factual question answering; our analysis of token scores supports this association
(Appendix~\ref{app:mechanism}), though model size does not determine temperature sensitivity in our experiments, where Qwen3-1.7B maintains its accuracy and the larger Llama-3.1-8B collapses.

\section{Experimental setup}
\label{sec:setup}

The study tests every combination of model, quantization level, decoding configuration, and temperature on two tasks. Table~\ref{tab:design} lists the grid and the additional experiments. We call one model at one quantization level, on one task
at one temperature, a \emph{cell}; we test the eight decoding configurations in every cell on the same fixed set of 50 items. Every sampler comparison in the paper therefore keeps the cell fixed and changes the decoding configuration, and every temperature comparison keeps the configuration fixed and changes only the temperature. We keep the other experimental settings fixed: one llama.cpp commit, each
model's official chat template rendered client-side, the native completion API, and
per-record seeds derived deterministically from the cell and configuration. An accuracy difference
between two runs is then attributable to the experimental factors, up to random variation from sampling that the bootstrap below quantifies.

\textbf{Decoding.} We test eight decoding configurations in each cell: a deterministic greedy baseline, plain
temperature sampling, and the four truncation rules of Section~\ref{sec:related}, each at the setting its source paper uses or recommends (top-$p$ 0.95 \citep{holtzman2020curious}, top-$k$ 40 \citep{radford2019gpt2}, min-$p$ 0.05 \citep{nguyen2024minp}, top-$n\sigma$ 1.0 \citep{tang2024topn}).
Because the order of temperature and truncation changes which tokens remain
(Section~\ref{sec:related}), we apply temperature scaling before truncation in the truncation configurations and add two ablation configurations that apply it after (the llama.cpp default) for top-$p$ and min-$p$, the
two rules whose retained set depends on that order. Temperatures $T \in \{0.7, 1.0, 1.3\}$ span the deployment
band. The default temperatures that models and engines set lie between 0.6 and 1.0 (Llama-3.1's
generation config sets 0.6, llama.cpp defaults to 0.8, the OpenAI and Anthropic APIs to
1.0),\footnote{Sources: Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct's \texttt{generation\_config.json},
llama.cpp's defaults, the OpenAI and Anthropic API references (July 2026).} so 0.7 and 1.0 represent common default temperatures and 1.3 lies just above them.
Every configuration that samples tokens runs
$K{=}3$ repetitions per item; greedy decoding is deterministic, so $K{=}1$.

\textbf{Tasks.} We evaluate GSM8K, grade-school math word problems
\citep{cobbe2021gsm8k} (0-shot chain-of-thought with the prompt of Meta's Llama
evaluation suite \citep{meta2024llamaevals}, numeric exact
match), and MMLU-Pro, ten-option multiple-choice questions across 14 categories
\citep{wang2024mmlupro} (0-shot chain-of-thought, letter exact match), $N{=}50$ items each, a 512-token budget on GSM8K and 1024 on MMLU-Pro (the cap), with parsers
faithful to lm-eval-harness \citep{gao2023evalharness}. Both tasks require a chain of reasoning ending in one exactly checkable answer, which
avoids variation from subjective grading and leaves a long generation in which coherence can
be lost. The two tasks differ
in generation length, with MMLU-Pro's generated reasoning containing roughly twice as many tokens as GSM8K's, a difference Section~\ref{sec:cliff} uses to test whether longer generations fail earlier. Both tasks grade a generation by
parsing a final answer out of the generated text, so the parser decides how a generation
that never reaches an answer is graded. We log the parse path of every record: strict
when the specified final-answer sentence is present (``The final answer is 26''; ``The
answer is (I)''), flexible when it is absent and the parser falls back to the last number
(GSM8K) or the last parenthesized option letter (MMLU-Pro), and failed when neither
exists, which grades as incorrect. Appendix~\ref{app:decomp} shows one generation for each extraction outcome and checks the rule against markdown formatting. Every
comparison in the study compares runs on the same fixed items.

\textbf{Models and quantization.} The grid holds seven instruction-tuned models from five
model families: Llama-3.1-8B \citep{grattafiori2024llama}, Mistral-7B-v0.3
\citep{jiang2023mistral}, Qwen2.5-7B \citep{yang2024qwen25}, Qwen3-1.7B/4B/8B
\citep{yang2025qwen3} (thinking off; a thinking-on spot-check in
Appendix~\ref{app:thinking}), and Gemma-3-12B \citep{gemma2025gemma3}. Each model runs at four quantization levels
(Q8\_0, Q6\_K, Q4\_K\_M, Q3\_K\_M), all imatrix GGUF conversions from a single provider
so the quantization axis is not confounded by the conversion procedure. Q8\_0 is the
primary precision for every temperature result in Section~\ref{sec:modelbound}; the
sampler comparisons of Section~\ref{sec:spread} pool the four levels, and
Appendix~\ref{app:quant} shows the drops level by level.

\textbf{Replication runs.} Two temperature-only runs reuse the grid's fixed items,
chat template, token budgets, and parser, with the greedy baseline and plain temperature
at the grid's three temperatures, $K{=}3$. The additional Llama evaluation adds two more Llama releases, Llama-3-8B \citep{meta2024llama3} and Llama-3.2-3B \citep{meta2024llama32},
at Q8\_0 through the same engine. The engine and precision run repeats the axis through
a second engine, HF transformers, with plain temperature sampling and no truncation or
penalties: Llama-3.2-3B on the official BF16 weights, Llama-3.1-8B in 8-bit integer
weights, and Qwen3-4B in BF16 as a robust control; Llama-3.2-3B also runs through
llama.cpp on the unquantized GGUF (Section~\ref{sec:replication}; settings and
provenance in Appendix~\ref{app:engine}).

\textbf{Statistical inference.} All confidence intervals come from a nonparametric
bootstrap ($B{=}2000$; 5000 for the comparisons of Appendix~\ref{app:bound}) clustered
on task items. We resample the 50 items instead of
individual generations, because the $K{=}3$ repetitions of one item share that item's
difficulty and treating them as independent would understate the uncertainty. For every comparison in the grid, both configurations use the same items, so the bootstrap is paired.

\textbf{Hardware and reproducibility.} The study totals about 211{,}000 graded
generations (Table~\ref{tab:design}). Hardware and the fixed llama.cpp commit are in Appendix~\ref{app:repro}; model-file
hashes are in the repository's \texttt{DOWNLOADS.md}.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Temperature sensitivity across models}
\label{sec:modelbound}

We begin with plain temperature sampling and no truncation, because the accuracy a
model loses when its full distribution is sampled at a higher temperature is exactly the
failure the truncation samplers of Section~\ref{sec:related} exist to prevent.
Figure~\ref{fig:collapse} plots the drop from $T{=}0.7$ to $T{=}1.3$ at Q8\_0 for every
model evaluated at both temperatures, and Table~\ref{tab:collapse} gives the values
together with the change in two measures of incoherent or incomplete generation: the share of generations that
run to the token cap, and the share that end in the specified final-answer sentence (a
strict parse, Section~\ref{sec:setup}).

Temperature sensitivity varies continuously across the models. The Qwen3 family and Gemma-3 drop
at most 5 points on either task with every CI covering zero; Qwen2.5 and Mistral drop
4 to 9 points, each with one task CI excluding zero; Llama-3.1-8B drops 30 and 38. Its
drop begins inside the default temperature range, since from $T{=}0.7$ to 1.0 its MMLU-Pro accuracy
already falls and its run-to-cap rate doubles (Appendix~\ref{app:decomp}). Two controls
rule out the obvious confounds. A low starting accuracy does not explain the collapse,
since Qwen3-1.7B scores 79\% on GSM8K, above Llama's 77\%, and does not collapse. Model age does not
explain it either, since Mistral-7B-v0.3 predates Llama-3.1 and is robust.

A single collapsing checkpoint could still be an effect specific to that release, so we repeat the temperature tests on two more Llama releases. Llama-3.2-3B collapses like
Llama-3.1-8B ($+38.0\,$pp on GSM8K, $+36.7$ on MMLU-Pro) and with the same
degeneration pattern, with its run-to-cap rate rising by 55 and 82 points. Llama-3-8B lies
between the two, with no measurable drop on GSM8K and $+16.7\,$pp on MMLU-Pro, the
longer task, a larger drop than any non-Llama model shows. Every model that loses more than 10 points is a
Llama-3 release, and the three releases produce the three largest MMLU-Pro drops in the
study. What sets them apart from the other four families is a question we discuss in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.

\subsection{Replication across item sets and implementations}
\label{sec:replication}

Every grid result comes from one engine, llama.cpp, on imatrix GGUF quantizations from
one provider, so a collapse could in principle be a property of the engine or of the
conversion rather than of the model. We therefore repeat the temperature tests on the same fixed item set through a second
engine, HF transformers, for both collapsing Llama checkpoints and for one robust control
(Table~\ref{tab:engine}). Llama-3.2-3B runs in native BF16 on the official weights and,
through llama.cpp, as the unquantized F16 GGUF; Llama-3.1-8B runs in 8-bit integer
weights, the highest precision our 16\,GB card holds; Qwen3-4B runs in BF16. The collapse
replicates on both Llama checkpoints and the control stays flat. In BF16 through
transformers Llama-3.2-3B loses 40.0\,pp on GSM8K and 36.0 on MMLU-Pro (Q8 through
llama.cpp: 38.0 and 36.7), 61 and 92\% of its $T{=}1.3$ generations run to the cap (55
and 92\%), and the paired difference between the two engines' drops is $+2.0$ [$-9.3$,
12.7] on GSM8K and $-0.7$ [$-9.3$, 8.0] on MMLU-Pro. The F16 GGUF loses 31.3 and 32.7.
Llama-3.1-8B in INT8 loses 43.3 and 42.7 (Q8: 30.0 and 38.0), with 54 and 95\% of its
$T{=}1.3$ generations at the cap; on GSM8K the INT8 drop exceeds the Q8 drop by 13.3
[2.7, 24.0]. Qwen3-4B in BF16 moves by 1.3\,pp on GSM8K and 0.7 on MMLU-Pro, as in its
Q8 and BF16 GGUF cells. These runs replicate the drop and its failure pattern. They do
not show that the engines are equivalent: the intervals on the differences span about
20\,pp, and the 8B GSM8K difference excludes zero. For Llama-3.2-3B the prompt strings
differ only by a date its template stamps into the system header, which changes no
token count; the other two arms reproduce every grid prompt hash
(Appendix~\ref{app:engine}).

\subsection{What fails when accuracy falls}
\label{sec:degeneration}

All analyses in this subsection use plain temperature sampling. An accuracy drop
can come from two failures: completed answers that are wrong, or generations that never
reach an answer. The answer-extraction outcome that Section~\ref{sec:setup} records for every generation separates the two, so we separate Llama's accuracy loss by extraction outcome
(Figure~\ref{fig:decomposition}; full decomposition and example outputs in
Appendix~\ref{app:decomp}).

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.78\textwidth]{fig2_decomposition}
\caption{Overall accuracy (solid) versus accuracy among well-formed, strict-parsed answers
(dashed) under plain temperature sampling; four models with different levels of temperature sensitivity (the three Qwen3 models maintain stable accuracy and are omitted for legibility). The dashed lines never fall. Among well-formed answers accuracy holds, and Llama's rises because its completed answers come from easier items (Section~\ref{sec:degeneration}).}
\label{fig:decomposition}
\end{figure*}
Nearly all of the drop is the second failure. At $T{=}1.3$, 53.8\% of Llama's GSM8K
generations and 89.2\% of its MMLU-Pro generations degenerate into token-level noise and
run to the token cap. A blinded audit of 152 sampled outputs, labelled without access to
model, temperature, or parser fields, supports this interpretation. Every Llama generation at
the cap at $T{=}1.3$ in the sample is incoherent, its generations at the cap at $T{=}0.7$ are coherent
reasoning cut short by the budget, and the parse failures at $T{=}0.7$ are formatting
failures (Appendix~\ref{app:decomp}). Among the generations that do produce a well-formed final answer (a strict parse: the specified final-answer sentence is present), Llama still scores
97.3\% on GSM8K at $T{=}1.3$. That number needs a caveat. Survival is selective, because easier items produce shorter generations and
shorter generations have less room to lose coherence, so the 97.3\% is measured on an
easier-than-average set of items. The matched comparison is greedy decoding on the same items for which sampling produced a well-formed answer, at the
same quantization level, which scores 97.3\%, against 78.0\% on all items. Among the generations that finish, then, accuracy at $T{=}1.3$ matches greedy decoding
on the same items (97.3 vs.\ 97.3\% on GSM8K; 76.9 vs.\ 78.8\% on MMLU-Pro). The comparison cannot say how the
incoherent generations would have scored had they finished. It shows that temperature
lowers the rate at which the model finishes and leaves the accuracy of the answers it
does finish unchanged.

The two task parsers classify the same failure differently. Degenerate text almost always contains a
number, so GSM8K's last-number fallback grades an incoherent generation as a wrong answer,
while MMLU-Pro's letter parser reports a parse failure. Temperature does not raise Llama's rate of wrong answers; it raises the rate of
generations that never reach one.

\subsection{Sampler gains depend on temperature sensitivity}
\label{sec:spread}
\label{sec:cliff}

This subsection varies the sampler. For each model, task, and temperature we compare
each of the six truncation configurations with plain temperature on the same items, as the
paired accuracy difference pooled over the four quantization levels, with an
item-clustered bootstrap CI (per-configuration accuracy grids in Appendix~\ref{app:grids}; all comparisons in
Appendix~\ref{app:bound}; a generalized estimating equation, a regression that accounts for the repeated observations of each item, fitted over the full grid reproduces every interval to within 0.6\,pp). At $T{=}0.7$ and 1.0, the default
temperatures, no configuration gains more than 3.3\,pp on any robust model, no pointwise upper 95\% limit
exceeds 7.5\,pp (a simultaneous bound on the largest gain over all 144 comparisons is 7.8),
135 of 144 point estimates lie within 3\,pp of plain temperature, and 6 intervals
exclude zero (2 above, 4 below), the number chance alone gives. At $T{=}1.3$, 22 of 72 comparisons lie above zero, all on Mistral, Qwen2.5, and Gemma-3,
the three non-Llama models with the largest temperature drops, with gains of 3 to
10\,pp, largest on the longer task; no Qwen3 comparison leaves zero at any temperature. Restricted to Q8\_0 alone (150 generations per cell instead of 600), no point estimate
at $T \le 1.0$ exceeds 4.7\,pp and no interval excludes zero, but the upper limits widen
to 13\,pp, so the pooled analysis gives the smaller upper bound and the Q8 analysis the
more conservative one. On the robust models no truncation rule gains more than the bound allows
at the default temperatures, and gains of a few points appear once a model has begun to
lose accuracy; on Llama the rules that exclude the most tokens already gain 6 to 11\,pp
on MMLU-Pro at $T{=}1.0$ (Appendix~\ref{app:bound}), where
its collapse has begun.

The order of temperature scaling and truncation changes accuracy by at most 4\,pp on the robust models at $T \le 1.0$ and by
up to 7\,pp on Mistral at $T{=}1.3$; on Llama, truncating before temperature scaling
preserves more accuracy at $T{=}1.3$
(top-$p$ 64\% $\to$ 73\% on GSM8K), as the effect of that order on the truncation threshold (Section~\ref{sec:related}) predicts (both orders in Appendix~\ref{app:tlast}).

\textbf{Cliff temperatures.} To see whether the robust models also collapse at higher
temperature, we test $T \in
\{1.3, 1.5, 1.7, 2.0\}$ on three models spanning the gradient of Figure~\ref{fig:collapse}:
Llama-3.1-8B, Qwen2.5-7B, and Gemma-3-12B (Q8, both tasks, $N{=}50$, $K{=}3$).
Figure~\ref{fig:cliff} and Table~\ref{tab:cliff} give the accuracy at each temperature
and the drops against each model's own $T{=}0.7$ baseline. All three reach a cliff inside
the tested range, at different temperatures. Taking the cliff as the lowest tested
temperature at which a model loses more than half its $T{=}0.7$ accuracy, the three models collapse on GSM8K in their Figure~\ref{fig:collapse} order:
Llama at 1.5, Qwen2.5 at 1.7, and Gemma at 2.0. At $T{=}1.5$ the CI of Llama's drop does not overlap the other two models', and at
$T{=}1.7$ Qwen2.5's does not overlap Gemma's. Within each model the cliff
comes at a lower temperature on MMLU-Pro, the task with roughly twice the generation length, as expected if each generated token carries a risk of making the continuation incoherent and that risk accumulates over a longer generation. There Llama has already collapsed at
$T{=}1.3$, and Qwen2.5 and Gemma both collapse at $T{=}1.5$.

Truncation raises the cliff temperature, and the more tokens a rule excludes the more it
raises it: top-$p$ raises the cliff by about 0.2, min-$p$ keeps 70\% accuracy at
$T{=}1.5$ on Llama GSM8K where plain temperature is at 9\%, and accuracy under
top-$n\sigma$ is unchanged up to $T{=}2.0$ on all three models. The strict-parse rate
tracks accuracy almost one-to-one across all tested temperatures, so the sharp fall in
accuracy coincides with a sharp fall in well-formed answers. This reconciles the sampler papers with the bound above. Min-$p$ and
top-$n\sigma$ are validated at temperatures of 1.5 to 3 \citep{nguyen2024minp,tang2024topn},
above every model's deployment range, where they do improve accuracy (Appendix~\ref{app:hight}).

\section{Discussion and conclusion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\textbf{For practitioners.} At deployment temperatures the consequential choice is the
model. On a robust one every configuration we test stays within 10 points and most
within 3; on a fragile one accuracy varies by 42 points across the same configurations. The fragility can be screened for
before deployment. On the fifty fixed items, the drop in strict-parse rate and the rise
in run-to-cap rate between $T{=}0.7$ and 1.3 separate the fragile models from the robust
ones in every run of this study (Table~\ref{tab:collapse}); we have not validated the
rule on models outside it. In production, an incoherent generation carries no well-formed
answer, so a check on the answer format catches it, while the rate of well-formed but
wrong answers changes little.

\textbf{For the sampler literature.} A sampler evaluation should say where its models'
cliffs sit, since a gain measured past one model's cliff need not exist on a model whose
cliff lies further out (Section~\ref{sec:cliff}).

\textbf{Toward a mechanism.} Two exploratory tests (Appendix~\ref{app:mechanism}) ask
what a fragile model does differently before coherence is lost. Greedy-path entropy and the
per-token chance of drawing a token from beyond the model's own top 20 both rank the
seven grid models' drops at Spearman $\rho = +0.93$, yet neither separates Llama-3.1
from Mistral, which is robust. A forced tail token does. Inserting the token ranked twentieth by probability into text generated with greedy decoding multiplies Llama's probability of subsequently sampling a low-probability token 2.3-fold, while no
robust model's rises by more than $+0.0015$ per position. With seven models and one
injection configuration these probes check the loss-of-coherence account for
consistency and do not test it causally, and what sets a model's cliff remains open.

Across nine models from five families, the choice of truncation sampler matters far less
at deployment temperatures than the model's own temperature sensitivity. Robust models
lose at most a few points under every sampler we test, while two Llama generations lose
up to 38 points under plain temperature and a third loses 17 on the longer task, in two
engines and at every precision we tried. Each model we test above the deployment range
collapses at its own temperature, Llama already inside that range; truncation raises that
temperature but changes little below it. These results argue for screening temperature
fragility before deployment rather than tuning the sampler after it.

\section*{Limitations}

Our sample is 50 items per task with $K{=}3$ repetitions per cell; the small item count
is what makes an experiment of 185{,}600 generations over every combination of factors feasible. Item-clustered bootstrap CIs
accompany every main estimate, and the main drops are 2 to 4 times their CI
widths. Mistral-7B's GSM8K accuracy is low (31\% at $T{=}0.7$), which limits the power to
detect a further drop; its MMLU-Pro drop is measured well above zero accuracy and its CI excludes
zero. The fixed MMLU-Pro subset is single-category (business) because the test split is
sorted by category; the stratified $N{=}50$ replication of Appendix~\ref{app:strat}
reproduces the ordering on fresh items, and it is a second sample of 50 items, so it
does not estimate the benchmark-wide effect. $T{=}1.3$ lies above the default
temperatures that models and engines set. It is a test temperature that bounds the
deployment range from above, and we do not expect deployments to use it.

The scope is open-weight instruction-tuned models of 1.7B to 12B parameters. Whether
larger or proprietary models share the Llama family's fragility is untested. Both tasks
are English, zero-shot, and objective, with a single exactly checkable answer, which is
what lets the parser measure degeneration. The original use case of truncation samplers, open-ended
generation with no checkable answer, lies outside this design, so our bound on sampler
gains does not extend to it. We replicate the results across engines and precisions for two Llama checkpoints and one control only, and the Llama-3.1-8B replication runs in 8-bit rather than native precision. We compare samplers only in llama.cpp, at one setting per rule, so the bound applies to those settings and that engine. We have not repeated the sampler comparisons on the stratified subset. Quantization covers the tested llama.cpp formats only, all produced with the same conversion procedure. Thinking mode is off
throughout the main grid; the thinking-on spot-check of Appendix~\ref{app:thinking}
agrees on GSM8K but shows a 10\,pp MMLU-Pro drop [2, 20] at $N{=}25$, so our bound does
not cover thinking mode.

Every fragile model we observe is a Llama-3 release. The evaluation of additional Llama releases shows the fragility spans three generations, which rules out an effect specific to one checkpoint, but the three share
training data and post-training methods, so they are not three independent pieces of
evidence. With one fragile family we cannot tell whether the fragility comes from a training or post-training choice made by Llama's developers or from something else these models have in common, and we cannot say how
common such families are.

Three analysis caveats. Accuracy conditional on a strict parse is subject to
survivorship; Section~\ref{sec:degeneration} bounds it with a matched greedy comparison
and does not remove it, and it says nothing about how the incoherent generations would
have scored. The bound of Section~\ref{sec:spread} is a pointwise 95\% upper limit on the
sampler effect at $N{=}50$ (7.8\,pp simultaneous). It does not show that the effect is
zero, and it does not establish equivalence within a smaller margin. The mechanism probes correlate
seven models and use one injection configuration (a rank-20 token at position 48, a
32-position window); Appendix~\ref{app:mechanism} presents them as consistency checks,
not a causal account, and the grid contains no base-versus-instruct or checkpoint axis,
so it cannot say what moves the cliff.

\section{Quantization}
\label{app:quant}
The results so far pool over the four quantization levels. For that to be safe,
quantization must not cost accuracy on its own, and it must not change what temperature
and the samplers do. Quantization costs little accuracy. From Q8\_0 down to Q3\_K\_M, greedy accuracy is
unchanged on every model of 7B parameters or more, and a real cost appears only on the
two smallest models, 7\,pp on Qwen3-4B and 9\,pp on Qwen3-1.7B. It changes nothing
about the collapse either. Recomputed separately at each level, the plain-temperature
drop of Section~\ref{sec:modelbound} shows Llama-3.1 collapsing at every level, with
drops of 30 to 47\,pp across levels and tasks, while no robust model exceeds 12\,pp at
any single level. Llama's sampler ordering at $T{=}1.3$ is identical at all four levels

\section{Answer agreement across repetitions}
\label{app:selfcons}
A second check separates robustness from determinism. A model with very peaked
next-token distributions would also hold its accuracy, because sampling would return the
greedy path every time and the three repetitions of an item would come out as near
copies. The repetitions show the opposite. No item's three repetitions are identical on any model, yet the robust models still
converge on the answer, 1.1 to 1.4 distinct parsed answers per item on GSM8K at
$T{=}1.3$ (Mistral, whose low GSM8K accuracy scatters its answers, is 2.5). Llama-3.1 rises from 1.5 to 2.3 under the same
change, as each repetition becomes incoherent on its own (Table~\ref{tab:selfcons}).
Neither quantization nor determinism explains the pattern.

\section{Mechanism probes}
\label{app:mechanism}
If the collapse is a loss of coherence process, fragile models should carry more per-token
uncertainty before any loss of coherence happens. To measure that uncertainty we decode the
100 fixed prompts (the 50 items of each task) greedily on every model at Q8 and record
the raw top-20 next-token probabilities at each position. Each prompt is summarized by
four statistics of these distributions: an entropy lower bound (only the top 20
probabilities are observed), the top-1 probability and its margin over the top-2, and
the fraction of positions where the top-1 probability falls below 0.5. We decode greedily so that the statistics describe the text each model produces
when no random draw is involved, before any random draw can become incoherent it.

Mean greedy-path entropy ranks the seven models' temperature drops at Spearman
$\rho = +0.93$ (flat-position fraction $+0.93$; top-1 margin $-0.93$; Figure~\ref{fig:mechanism}). With seven models a rank correlation is fragile, so we report an item-clustered
bootstrap interval, $[+0.54, +0.96]$, and add an exact
permutation test ($p = 0.007$) and leave-one-model-out ranges. The summaries cannot separate
one pair, though. Llama-3.1 and Mistral are nearly tied on every statistic we record,
0.33 versus 0.32 nats of entropy and $1.0 \times 10^{-3}$ probability mass beyond the
top 20 for both, yet Llama drops 40.8\,pp on MMLU-Pro and Mistral 8.0. Uncertainty on the greedy path predicts which models drop most, but not by how much.

The probe also estimates the per-token risk that drives a loss of coherence. For each
position we compute the probability that sampling at $T{=}1.3$ picks a token from beyond
the model's own top 20; we call this the escape hazard (the estimator and its tail assumption are stated
below). Llama's is 0.0033 per position
and Mistral's 0.0034, about 0.85 expected tail draws per 256 positions for both, and
across the seven models the hazard ranks the drops at the same $\rho = +0.93$ (bootstrap
$[+0.57, +0.96]$). Generation lengths show that a incoherent generation does not recover. On every robust
model the length distribution of
finished generations is unchanged from $T{=}0.7$ to $T{=}1.3$ (90th-percentile ratio
0.85 to 1.19), and Llama's finished generations at $T{=}1.3$ are \emph{shorter}, because
only the short ones finish (Section~\ref{sec:degeneration}). Llama and Mistral therefore draw tail tokens equally often, and a generation that
becomes incoherent does not come back, so what separates them must be how often a tail token starts
a loss of coherence, that is, what happens after that token is in the context.

A perturbation experiment measures that directly. For each model and fixed prompt we
greedy-decode a 48-token prefix, replace the next token with the model's own rank-20
candidate at that position (median raw probability $2 \times 10^{-9}$, a genuine tail
draw), and continue greedily for 256 tokens, alongside a control continuation of the
unedited prefix. We then compare the two continuations' escape hazard over the 32 positions after
the injection.

Beyond the injected token itself, the continuations show no visible damage. They stay
coherent,
and Llama's reach the token cap no more often than its controls (14.0\% for both on
GSM8K). The difference is in the hazard. Llama's rises to 2.3 times its baseline
($+0.0042$ per position), Mistral's to 1.35 times ($+0.0012$), and no robust model gains more than $+0.0015$ (the absolute increase rather than the ratio, for the reason given below). This per-model increase ranks the temperature drops at
$\rho = +0.89$ (bootstrap $[+0.39, +0.96]$, permutation $p = 0.012$; the entropy
increase, $+0.86$).  With seven models these probes check the
loss of coherence account for consistency and do not confirm it causally. In short, Llama and Mistral draw tail tokens at the same rate, and what sets Llama
apart is how much one tail token raises the chance of the next.

Figure~\ref{fig:mechanism} plots greedy-path entropy against the temperature drop.
Table~\ref{tab:mechanism} consolidates the per-model mechanism evidence of
Appendix~\ref{app:mechanism}: greedy-path entropy lower bound and flat-position fraction
(top-1 $<$ 0.5) from the summary probe; the temperature-scaled beyond-top-20 escape

\section{Reproducibility}
\label{app:repro}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Inference stack.} llama.cpp at commit \texttt{5aba5364} (server build
    9456), native \texttt{/completion} endpoint, CUDA builds of the same commit per GPU
    architecture (sm\_75 for RTX 2080~Ti, sm\_120 for RTX 5070~Ti). Server-side BOS
    insertion is disabled; the client-rendered chat template owns the special tokens, and
    each record stores the SHA-256 of the exact rendered prompt.
  \item \textbf{Quantizations.} Every quantized checkpoint is a Bartowski imatrix GGUF
    (one provider, one recipe, so the quantization axis is not confounded by recipe).
    SHA-256 hashes of all 31 model files are pinned in the repository
    (\texttt{DOWNLOADS.md}).
  \item \textbf{Decoding.} Exactly one truncation parameter per request; the sampler
    chain order is pinned per configuration through the server's \texttt{samplers} array and
    recorded in every record. At the pinned commit the server instantiates only the
    samplers named in that array and then samples from the result, so its own defaults
    for unlisted samplers (top-$k$ 40, top-$p$ 0.95, min-$p$ 0.05, repetition penalty
    1.0) are inert; no penalty, DRY, XTC, or logit-bias parameter is ever sent. Sampler values: top-$p$
    0.95, top-$k$ 40, min-$p$ 0.05, top-$n\sigma$ 1.0 (each method's recommended value).
  \item \textbf{Seeding.} Every generation's seed is derived deterministically from
    (global seed 0, model, quantization, sampler, temperature, task, item, repetition).
    Greedy decoding is the deterministic $T{=}0$ baseline with $K{=}1$.
  \item \textbf{Tasks.} GSM8K (openai/gsm8k, test split, first 50 items) and MMLU-Pro
    (TIGER-Lab/MMLU-Pro, test split, first 50 items; stratified replication in
    Appendix~\ref{app:strat}). Parsers mirror lm-eval-harness; parse failures grade as
    incorrect and are reported.
  \item \textbf{Records.} Each generation is one JSONL record carrying the full identity
    tuple, the request parameters, the raw output, the parse path, and throughput
    covariates; runs are resumable by record id. Batched serving ($\le 8$ slots) is
    seed-logged but not bit-exact.
  \item \textbf{Code and data.} The full pipeline, configs, analysis scripts, and
    every generation record are in an anonymized repository at
    \url{https://anonymous.4open.science/r/decoding-robustness-027C}.
\end{itemize}
