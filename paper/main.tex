\begin{abstract}
Truncation samplers such as top-$p$, min-$p$, and top-$n\sigma$ exist to prevent the
accuracy collapse of high-temperature sampling. We ask whether the collapse itself is
universal. Using a single controlled pipeline, we run seven instruction-tuned models from
five families (1.7B to 12B parameters) through a factorial grid of 4 quantization levels,
8 sampler arms, and 2 tasks, plus a temperature-only cell on two further Llama
generations, about 204{,}000 generations in total. At deployment temperatures ($T \le
1.3$), sampler choice barely matters: outside one family, the per-model accuracy spread
across samplers is 1.3 to 10.0 percentage points (pp), and even that overstates the true
differences. The exception is the Llama family. Under plain temperature sampling at
$T{=}1.3$, Llama-3.1-8B loses 34\,pp (GSM8K) and 41\,pp (MMLU-Pro), Llama-3.2-3B
collapses the same way, and Llama-3-8B loses 17\,pp on MMLU-Pro; every model outside the
family stays within 8\,pp. The collapse is a termination failure: generations derail
midway and never reach an answer, while accuracy conditional on producing a well-formed
answer stays at the greedy level in every cell (97\% for Llama-3.1-8B on GSM8K at
$T{=}1.3$). A temperature ladder up to $T{=}2.0$ locates each model's cliff and shows
that truncation samplers only begin to matter past it. A logit probe and a forced-token
perturbation experiment trace the fragility to a self-reinforcing instability: one
off-distribution token in context multiplies the fragile model's chance of drawing the
next one. Practitioners tuning a current model can leave the sampler alone.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Most deployed language models generate text by sampling. At each step the model assigns
a probability to every candidate next token; the decoder either takes the most probable
one (greedy decoding) or draws at random from the distribution. Systems sample because
greedy decoding returns one fixed continuation per prompt, and in open-ended text that
continuation tends to be repetitive \citep{holtzman2020curious}. A temperature parameter
scales the randomness of the draw: raising it moves probability from the model's
preferred tokens toward rare ones.

The rare tokens are where sampling goes wrong. A model's probability estimates are least
reliable in the tail of its distribution, so a high-temperature draw can select a token
that fits the context poorly \citep{holtzman2020curious,hewitt2022truncation}. The
selected token then becomes context for every later step, and the continuation can
degenerate into incoherent text. Truncation samplers exist to prevent this degeneration:
they delete the tail of the distribution before the draw and renormalize what remains.
The rules differ in where they cut the tail; top-$k$, nucleus (top-$p$), min-$p$, and
top-$n\sigma$ sampling are the four we test, and Section~\ref{sec:related} describes each
\citep{fan2018hierarchical,holtzman2020curious,nguyen2024minp,tang2024topn}. All of these
were developed for open-ended generation, such as story writing and dialogue, where
output variety is the goal \citep{zhou2024decoding}.

The same samplers are now evaluated on tasks with one checkable answer. Deployed
assistants keep sampling on for every request, so math word problems and multiple-choice
exams are also decoded at nonzero temperature \citep{song2024goodbad,renze2024temperature}.
On such tasks a decoding method is scored by accuracy: each generation is right or wrong,
where open-ended generation needs a human or model judge \citep{zhou2024decoding}. Min-$p$,
for example, reports accuracy gains over top-$p$ on GSM8K at high temperature
\citep{nguyen2024minp}.

Those accuracy claims drew a critique. \citet{schaeffer2025minp} asked whether any one
sampler beats the others once statistical controls are in place. Whichever sampler wins
that comparison, the comparison itself takes the collapse for granted: it assumes that
raising the temperature eventually ruins every model's accuracy, and asks only which
sampler limits the damage. We test the premise:
\textbf{is the collapse itself a property of decoding, or of the model?}

Across nine instruction-tuned models from five families, run through one controlled
pipeline, the answer is: of the model. The six models from outside the Llama family hold
their accuracy at deployment temperatures under every sampler we test, staying within 8
points of their low-temperature scores. Every larger loss belongs to a Llama:
Llama-3.1-8B and Llama-3.2-3B lose 34 to 41 accuracy points under plain temperature
sampling, and Llama-3-8B loses 17 on the longer task. Sampler choice, the variable the
truncation literature tunes, is worth at most a few points anywhere below each model's
collapse temperature.

Our contributions:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{A single-pipeline factorial study.} Seven instruction-tuned models, four
    GGUF quantization levels (model weights stored at reduced precision) plus a BF16
    anchor, six decoding methods plus two ablation arms that swap the order in which
    temperature and truncation are applied, and $T \in \{0.7, 1.0, 1.3\}$, on GSM8K and
    MMLU-Pro ($N{=}50$ items, $K{=}3$ repetitions): about 204{,}000 generations, with
    item-clustered bootstrap confidence intervals throughout, plus a follow-up
    temperature-only cell on two more Llama generations.
  \item \textbf{Robustness is model-bound, and the fragility runs in one family.}
    Every collapse we observe is a Llama, across three of its generations
    (Section~\ref{sec:modelbound}). On the robust models the sampler is worth at most a
    few points, and Section~\ref{sec:spread} shows even those few points overstate the
    true differences.
  \item \textbf{The collapse is a termination failure.} Conditional on emitting
    a well-formed final answer, accuracy is flat in temperature everywhere. What varies is
    the probability of derailing into unparseable text mid-generation
    (Section~\ref{sec:degeneration}).
  \item \textbf{A cliff map from $T{=}1.3$ to $2.0$.} Every model collapses somewhere;
    what distinguishes models is where. On GSM8K, Llama collapses by $T{=}1.5$, Qwen2.5
    by 1.7, and Gemma-3 by 2.0, and truncation samplers shift each cliff right in order
    of tail-cutting strength (top-$n\sigma$ $\gg$ min-$p$ $\gg$ top-$p$). The gains the
    sampler papers report are real, but they appear past the cliff, above deployment
    temperatures (Section~\ref{sec:cliff}).
  \item \textbf{Supporting results.} Quantization from Q8 down to Q3 causes no collapse
    on any model and does not change which sampler ranks best
    (Section~\ref{sec:quant}). A logit probe and a forced-token experiment
    (Section~\ref{sec:mechanism}) locate the fragility: every model draws rare
    off-distribution tokens at the same per-token rate, but one injected tail token
    multiplies Llama's subsequent escape hazard 2.3-fold while every robust model absorbs
    it.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Truncation samplers.}
Top-$k$ sampling draws from only the $k$ most probable tokens; it was introduced for
story generation, where likelihood-maximizing decoding produced generic and repetitive
text \citep{fan2018hierarchical}. \citet{holtzman2020curious} showed that a fixed $k$
fits poorly because the next-token distribution is sometimes peaked and sometimes flat,
and proposed nucleus sampling, which keeps the smallest set of tokens whose probabilities
sum to a target $p$. Min-$p$ keeps only the tokens whose probability is at least a set fraction of the top
token's, so the cut is tight where the model is confident and loose where it is
uncertain \citep{nguyen2024minp}.
Top-$n\sigma$ cuts in logit space instead, keeping tokens within $n$ standard deviations
of the top logit; temperature rescales all logits equally, so the surviving set does not
change with temperature \citep{tang2024topn}. Further refinements of the cut exist,
among them typical sampling and epsilon and eta sampling
\citep{meister2023typical,hewitt2022truncation}. All of these rules target the same failure:
incoherent text drawn from the tail at high temperature. \citet{welleck2020consistency}
proved the worst case of that failure, showing that standard decoders can place
probability on generations that never terminate. In contrast to the sampler papers, which
compare truncation rules against each other, we test whether the failure they all guard
against occurs at deployment temperatures at all. Outside the Llama family it does not,
and on the Llamas it takes the non-terminating form: generations run to the token cap
without reaching an answer (Section~\ref{sec:degeneration}).

\paragraph{Reproducibility of sampler gains.}
\citet{schaeffer2025minp} reanalyzed min-$p$ on nine models and 31 temperatures, giving
every sampler an equal number of hyperparameter settings, and found its reported gains
``largely indistinguishable'' from the other samplers'. Part of the gains traced to the
order in which temperature and truncation are applied. The order matters because the
truncation threshold is computed on whichever distribution the rule sees: applied before
temperature, the rule cuts the model's original, sharp distribution; applied after, it
cuts one that temperature has already flattened, so the same nominal setting keeps a
different set of tokens. We adopt both lessons: we report
statistical uncertainty throughout and run the two chain orders as explicit arms
(Section~\ref{sec:setup}). That critique pooled its models together, because its question
was about the sampler. Our question is about the model, so we keep every model separate
and ask whether the collapse happens on each one. \citet{zhou2024decoding} sweeps
decoding hyperparameters on open-ended generation with
quality and diversity metrics, while our tasks have one checkable answer.
\citet{shi2024decoding} compare decoding methods on closed-ended tasks and report that
the best method changes with the task, the model, and the quantization level; the
comparison is again among methods, and it leaves our premise question open.
\citet{song2024goodbad} documents per-model gaps between greedy decoding and sampling on
reasoning benchmarks and treats the gaps as evaluation noise to report; we treat the gap
as the object of study and trace it to a termination failure in one model family.

\paragraph{Robustness to decoding randomness.}
\citet{renze2024temperature} sampled temperatures from 0.0 to 1.6 on multiple-choice
problem solving and found no statistically significant effect on accuracy between 0.0 and
1.0. Our results replicate that null
inside the deployment band. But the null is pooled across models, and pooling hides the
outlier: our per-model ladder shows the heterogeneity beginning just past $T{=}1.0$,
where Llama-3.1-8B falls while every other model holds (Section~\ref{sec:modelbound}).
The nearest study is \citet{grover2026reliability}, which also asks what makes a model
robust to decoding randomness and concludes that instruction tuning is the primary
determinant. Our data refine that conclusion: all seven of our models are
instruction-tuned and one still collapses, so instruction tuning cannot be the whole
answer. Four design differences let our grid observe what theirs cannot:
\begin{itemize}
  \item their sweep stops at $T{=}1.0$, and the Llama collapse happens between 1.0
    and 1.3;
  \item their robust models score 6 to 30\% on synthetic tasks, so there is little
    accuracy left to lose;
  \item their instruct-versus-base contrast changes quantization at the same time (4-bit
    versus full precision), while we sweep quantization as its own axis;
  \item they test no truncation samplers.
\end{itemize}
\citet{fastowski2025confidence} connect the entropy of a model's token distributions to
its temperature sensitivity on factual question answering and report that larger models
are more stable. Our logit probe supports the entropy link (Section~\ref{sec:mechanism}).
Size does not decide the outcome in our grid, because Qwen3-1.7B holds where the larger
Llama-3.1-8B collapses.

\section{Experimental setup}
\label{sec:setup}

The study is a factorial grid over four axes: model, quantization level, decoding arm,
and temperature, crossed with two tasks. Every claim in the paper is a comparison between
cells of this grid, so everything outside the axes is pinned: one llama.cpp commit, each
model's official chat template rendered client-side, the native completion API, and
per-record seeds derived deterministically from the cell identity. An accuracy difference between
two cells is then attributable to the axes, up to sampling noise that the bootstrap below
quantifies.

\textbf{Decoding.} Each cell runs eight arms: a deterministic greedy anchor, plain
temperature sampling, and the four truncation rules of Section~\ref{sec:related}, each at
the setting its source paper uses or recommends: top-$p$ 0.95
\citep{holtzman2020curious}, top-$k$ 40 \citep{radford2019gpt2}, min-$p$ 0.05
\citep{nguyen2024minp}, top-$n\sigma$ 1.0 \citep{tang2024topn}.
Because the order of temperature and truncation changes which tokens survive
(Section~\ref{sec:related}), we pin one order, temperature first, across the truncation
arms, and add two ablation arms that instead apply temperature last (the llama.cpp
default) for top-$p$ and min-$p$. The other two rules need no ablation arm: temperature
scaling preserves the token ranking, so top-$k$ keeps the same set under either order,
and top-$n\sigma$ keeps the same set at any temperature by construction
(Section~\ref{sec:related}). Temperatures $T \in \{0.7, 1.0, 1.3\}$ span the deployment
band: the defaults that models and engines ship sit between 0.6 and 1.0 (Llama-3.1's
generation config sets 0.6, llama.cpp defaults to 0.8, the OpenAI and Anthropic APIs to
1.0), so 0.7 and 1.0 bracket common practice and 1.3 sits just above it.
Section~\ref{sec:cliff} extends the axis to $T{=}2.0$. Every stochastic arm runs
$K{=}3$ repetitions per item; greedy decoding is deterministic, so $K{=}1$.

\textbf{Tasks.} We evaluate GSM8K, grade-school math word problems
\citep{cobbe2021gsm8k} (0-shot chain-of-thought with the prompt of Meta's Llama
evaluation suite \citep{grattafiori2024llama}, numeric exact
match), and MMLU-Pro, ten-option multiple-choice questions across 14 categories
\citep{wang2024mmlupro} (0-shot chain-of-thought, letter exact match), $N{=}50$ items
each, with parsers faithful to lm-eval-harness. Both tasks ask the model to generate a
chain of reasoning and land on one exactly checkable answer, and the study needs both
properties: exact-match grading keeps effects of a few points free of judge noise, and a
derailment can only show up if there is a long generation to derail. The two tasks differ
in generation length, with MMLU-Pro's chains running roughly twice as long as GSM8K's, a
contrast Section~\ref{sec:cliff} uses to test whether longer generations fail earlier. Both tasks grade a generation by
parsing a final answer out of the generated text, so the parser decides how a generation
that never reaches an answer is graded. We therefore log the parse path of every record
(strict final-answer sentence, flexible fallback, or failure) and report the parse
failure rate as a metric of its own; a failed parse grades as incorrect.
Section~\ref{sec:degeneration} shows that this metric carries the main result. The frozen
MMLU-Pro subset is the head of the test split, and the split is sorted by category, so
all 50 items come from one category (business); every within-study contrast holds items
fixed, and a stratified replication reproduces the headline result
(Section~\ref{sec:modelbound}, Appendix~\ref{app:strat}).

\textbf{Models and quantization.} The grid holds seven instruction-tuned models from five
model families: Llama-3.1-8B \citep{grattafiori2024llama}, Mistral-7B-v0.3
\citep{jiang2023mistral}, Qwen2.5-7B \citep{yang2024qwen25}, Qwen3-1.7B/4B/8B
\citep{yang2025qwen3} (thinking off; a thinking-on spot-check in
Appendix~\ref{app:thinking}), and Gemma-3-12B \citep{gemma2025gemma3}. A
follow-up temperature-only cell adds two more Llama generations, Llama-3-8B and
Llama-3.2-3B (Section~\ref{sec:modelbound}). Each model runs at four quantization levels
(Q8\_0, Q6\_K, Q4\_K\_M, Q3\_K\_M), all imatrix GGUF conversions from a single provider
so the quantization axis is not confounded by the conversion
recipe. A BF16 anchor on Qwen3-4B matches Q8 within noise, which lets Q8 stand in for
full precision.

\textbf{Statistical inference.} All confidence intervals come from a nonparametric
bootstrap ($B{=}2000$) clustered on task items: we resample the 50 items rather than
individual generations, because the $K{=}3$ repetitions of one item share that item's
difficulty and treating them as independent would understate the uncertainty. Where a
comparison shares items across its two sides, which is everywhere in the grid, the
bootstrap is paired.

\textbf{Hardware.} Generation ran on RTX 2080~Ti (11\,GB) nodes of a university Slurm
cluster and one RTX 5070~Ti (16\,GB) workstation for the VRAM-heavy cells and the
follow-up runs, with per-architecture CUDA builds of the same pinned commit; the study
totals about 204{,}000 graded generations. Full reproducibility details, including
commit and file hashes, are in Appendix~\ref{app:repro}.

\section{Results}

\subsection{The collapse is model-bound}
\label{sec:modelbound}

We begin with plain temperature sampling, no truncation: whatever accuracy the intact
tail loses as temperature rises is the failure the truncation samplers of
Section~\ref{sec:related} exist to prevent. Figure~\ref{fig:collapse} gives the drop
from $T{=}0.7$ to $T{=}1.3$, pooled over quantization levels; Table~\ref{tab:collapse}
in Appendix~\ref{app:grids} gives the exact values, and Section~\ref{sec:quant} shows
quantization produces no collapse on any model.

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.82\textwidth]{fig1_collapse}
\caption{The pure-temperature collapse is model-bound: accuracy drop $T0.7 \to T1.3$,
points with 95\% item-clustered bootstrap CIs.}
\label{fig:collapse}
\end{figure*}

The models do not split cleanly into robust and fragile. The Qwen3 family is a tight
null, Qwen2.5, Gemma, and Mistral drop 2 to 8\,pp with at least one task CI excluding
zero, and Llama drops 34 to 41\,pp. Three controls rule out the obvious confounds. It is
not accuracy headroom: Qwen3-1.7B (74\% on GSM8K) sits in Llama's accuracy regime (78\%)
with the opposite temperature behavior. Model age does not explain it either:
Mistral-7B-v0.3 predates Llama-3.1 and is robust. The item subset is not driving it: the
stratified MMLU-Pro replication gives Llama $+43.3\,$pp [31.3, 55.3], Qwen2.5 $+6.0$, and
Gemma $+7.3$.

A single collapsing checkpoint could still be a quirk of that release rather than a
family trait, so a follow-up cell repeats the pure-temperature axis on two more Llama
generations, Llama-3-8B and Llama-3.2-3B (Q8, same frozen items, $K{=}3$;
Table~\ref{tab:lineage}, Appendix~\ref{app:lineage}). Llama-3.2-3B collapses
like Llama-3.1-8B ($+38.0\,$pp [29.3, 47.3] on GSM8K, $+36.7$ [26.7, 46.0] on MMLU-Pro)
and reproduces the degeneration signature of Section~\ref{sec:degeneration}: about half
of all generations run to the token cap, and accuracy among well-formed answers stays
at or above the greedy level. Llama-3-8B sits between the two: a null on GSM8K
($+4.0$ [$-2.7$, 11.3]) but $+16.7\,$pp [9.3, 24.0] on MMLU-Pro, the longer task, a
larger drop than any non-Llama model shows in Figure~\ref{fig:collapse}. Three Llama
generations produce the three largest MMLU-Pro drops in the study, so the fragility is
a family trait; what changed in the family's post-training is the open question of
Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.

\subsection{Sampler choice barely matters before the cliff}
\label{sec:spread}

The previous subsection varied temperature with the sampler fixed; this one holds
temperature at $T{=}1.3$, the top of the deployment band, and varies the sampler, the
axis the truncation literature tunes. The maximum minus minimum accuracy across the seven
stochastic sampler arms is 1.3 to 10.0\,pp on every robust model
(Figure~\ref{fig:spread} and full grids in Appendix~\ref{app:grids}), and even those numbers
overstate the axis: seven equally good samplers would still show a positive spread,
because the maximum picks the estimate that got lucky and the minimum the one that did
not. The true sampler differences are smaller still. Llama's spread of 36.7\,pp (GSM8K) and 41.8\,pp
(MMLU-Pro) measures something else. Its arms range from plain temperature, which has
already collapsed at $T{=}1.3$, to top-$n\sigma$, which truncates hard enough to hold
(Section~\ref{sec:cliff}), so on Llama the spread records how much of the collapse each
rule prevents.

Chain order (temperature first versus last) is a no-op on the robust models
($\pm$1--2\,pp). On Llama at $T{=}1.3$ it does matter: temperature-last truncation is
more protective (top-$p$ 64\% $\to$ 73\% on GSM8K), as the threshold mechanics of
Section~\ref{sec:related} predict; both orders are in Appendix~\ref{app:tlast}.

\subsection{The collapse is degeneration, not wrong answers}
\label{sec:degeneration}

An accuracy drop can come from two failures: completed answers that are wrong, or
generations that never reach an answer. The parse path that Section~\ref{sec:setup} logs
for every record separates the two, so we decompose Llama's collapse along it
(Figure~\ref{fig:decomposition} and full decomposition in Appendix~\ref{app:decomp}).
Nearly all of the drop is the second failure: at $T{=}1.3$, 53.8\% of Llama's GSM8K
generations and 89.2\% of its MMLU-Pro generations derail into token-level noise and run
to the token cap. Among the generations that do produce a well-formed final answer (a
strict parse: the generation ends with the pinned final-answer sentence), Llama still scores
97.3\% on GSM8K at $T{=}1.3$. That number needs one correction before it can be taken at
face value: survival is selective, because easier items produce shorter generations and
shorter generations have less room to derail. The fair baseline is therefore greedy
decoding restricted to the same surviving items, which scores 95.8\% (against 76.0\% over
all items). Conditional on surviving, then, accuracy at $T{=}1.3$ matches greedy decoding
(97.3 vs.\ 95.8\% on GSM8K; 76.9 vs.\ 78.8\% on MMLU-Pro).

The two tasks disguise one failure mode differently: degenerate text almost always
contains a number, so the GSM8K flexible last-number fallback grades a derailed
generation as a wrong answer while the MMLU-Pro letter parser reports a parse failure.
The underlying event is the same: the generation derails before it reaches an answer.

\subsection{The cliff map: robustness is cliff location}
\label{sec:cliff}

Section~\ref{sec:modelbound} leaves open whether the robust models lack the failure or
merely place it beyond the deployment band, so we extend the temperature axis to $T \in
\{1.3, 1.5, 1.7, 2.0\}$ on three models spanning the gradient of Figure~\ref{fig:collapse}:
Llama-3.1-8B, Qwen2.5-7B, and Gemma-3-12B (Q8, both tasks, $N{=}50$, $K{=}3$).
Figure~\ref{fig:cliff} plots the accuracy trajectories; Table~\ref{tab:cliff} in
Appendix~\ref{app:hight} gives each
model's drop against its own $T{=}0.7$ anchor. The answer is the second: every model has
a cliff, and what distinguishes the models is where it sits. On GSM8K the three models
collapse in their Figure~\ref{fig:collapse} order, with cliffs roughly 0.2 apart in
temperature, and the confidence intervals separate as the ladder climbs: from $T{=}1.7$
the three models' drops are pairwise disjoint. Within each model the cliff
sits earlier on MMLU-Pro, the task with roughly twice the generation length, exactly what
a compounding per-token derail risk predicts: there Llama has already collapsed at
$T{=}1.3$, and Qwen2.5 and Gemma fall together at $T{=}1.5$.

Truncation shifts the cliff right in order of tail-cutting strength: top-$p$ buys about
0.2 in temperature, min-$p$ holds 70\% at $T{=}1.5$ on Llama GSM8K where plain
temperature is at 9\%, and top-$n\sigma$ is flat to $T{=}2.0$ on all three models,
consistent with its construction: the surviving token set is
temperature-invariant. The strict-parse rate tracks accuracy almost one-to-one along the
whole ladder, so the cliff \emph{is} the degeneration cliff. This reconciles the sampler
papers with our null result: min-$p$ and top-$n\sigma$ are validated at temperatures of
1.5 to 3, past every model's deployment range, where their gains are real and ordered
top-$n\sigma$ $\gg$ min-$p$ $\gg$ top-$p$ (the $T{=}2$--3 probe in
Appendix~\ref{app:hight}).

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.88\textwidth]{fig3_cliff}
\caption{The cliff map. Accuracy versus temperature per sampler (the $T \le 1.0$ points
come from the main matrix; all at Q8). Every model has a cliff; where it sits is the
model property. Truncation shifts the cliff right by tail-cutting strength.}
\label{fig:cliff}
\end{figure*}

\subsection{Quantization and answer agreement}
\label{sec:quant}

The results so far pool over the four quantization levels. For that to be safe,
quantization must not cost accuracy on its own, and it must not change what temperature
and the samplers do. This subsection checks both. The direct cost is small. From Q8\_0 down
to Q3\_K\_M, greedy accuracy is unchanged on every model of 7B parameters or more: all
confidence intervals span zero (Figure~\ref{fig:quant}, Appendix~\ref{app:quant}). A real cost appears only on the
two smallest models and grows as the model shrinks, 7\,pp [1, 13] on Qwen3-4B and 9\,pp
[0, 18] on Qwen3-1.7B.

The interaction is what pooling actually depends on, so we recompute the
plain-temperature drop of Section~\ref{sec:modelbound} separately at each quantization level
(Table~\ref{tab:quantdrop}, Appendix~\ref{app:quant}). The result is the same at every
level: Llama-3.1 collapses everywhere, with drops of 30 to 47\,pp across levels and
tasks, while no robust model exceeds 12\,pp at any single level. The BF16 anchor on
Qwen3-4B, running at full precision, shows the same null drop as its quantized versions,
and Llama's sampler ordering at $T{=}1.3$ is identical at all four levels. Quantization
shifts baseline accuracy on small models; it does not create the collapse, remove it, or
change which sampler helps.

A second check addresses a different worry: that the robustness of
Section~\ref{sec:modelbound} is nothing more than determinism. Flat accuracy across
temperatures is also what we would see if temperature never really changed the output,
because the model's next-token distribution is peaked enough that sampling returns
nearly the greedy path every time. The $K{=}3$ repetitions of the matrix distinguish the
two readings: each item carries
three independently sampled generations under identical settings. If the robust models
were merely deterministic, the three generations would be near copies of one another.
Instead, the token sequences differ across repetitions, so temperature does change what
these models write. The question is whether it also changes their answers. For each item
we count the distinct parsed answers among its three repetitions, 1 meaning all three
agree and 3 meaning all three disagree; this is the agreement that self-consistency
decoding \citep{wang2023selfconsistency} exploits by majority vote, used here as a
diagnostic.

On the robust models the answers converge: 1.1 to 1.4 distinct answers per item on GSM8K
at $T{=}1.3$, and raising temperature from 0.7 to 1.3 moves the statistic by at most
$+0.17$ (Table~\ref{tab:selfcons}, Appendix~\ref{app:selfcons}). These models sample
genuinely different token sequences and still land on the same answer. Llama-3.1 rises
from 1.5 to 2.3 under the same change: its three repetitions increasingly disagree with
one another, as expected when each one derails independently. Mistral sits near 2.2 at
both temperatures, and its scatter has a different source: at a greedy GSM8K accuracy of
31\%, most repetitions are wrong, and wrong repetitions rarely agree on the same wrong
number at any temperature. The shift attributable to temperature is $+0.27$.

\subsection{Toward a mechanism: per-token uncertainty ranks with fragility}
\label{sec:mechanism}

If the collapse is a derailment process, fragile models should carry more per-token
uncertainty before any derailment happens. To measure that uncertainty we decode the
100 frozen prompts (the 50 items of each task) greedily on every model at Q8 and record
the raw top-20 next-token probabilities at each position. Each prompt is summarized by
four statistics of these distributions: an entropy lower bound (only the top 20
probabilities are observed), the top-1 probability and its margin over the top-2, and
the fraction of positions where the top-1 probability falls below 0.5. We decode greedily so that every model is measured on its own modal path;
the statistics then describe the undisturbed trajectory, unaffected by any sampled
derailment.

Mean greedy-path entropy ranks the seven models' temperature drops at Spearman
$\rho = +0.96$ (flat-position fraction $+0.96$; top-1 margin $-0.96$;
Figure~\ref{fig:mechanism}, Appendix~\ref{app:mechanism}): the flatter a model's next-token distributions on its own
greedy path, the more accuracy it loses to temperature. The summaries cannot separate
one pair, though. Llama-3.1 and Mistral are nearly tied on every statistic we record,
0.33 versus 0.32 nats of entropy and $1.0 \times 10^{-3}$ probability mass beyond the
top 20 for both, yet Llama drops 40.8\,pp on MMLU-Pro and Mistral 8.0. Uncertainty on
the undisturbed path predicts the ordering of the drops and leaves their size
unexplained.

Generation lengths rule out one candidate mechanism, derail-and-recover, in which the
model wanders off for a stretch, finds its way back, and loses accuracy only when the
detour corrupts the answer: a recovered detour leaves extra tokens behind, so
terminated generations at $T{=}1.3$ should be longer than at $T{=}0.7$.
On every robust model their length distribution is instead essentially unchanged
(90th-percentile ratio 0.85 to 1.19 across models and tasks; Table~\ref{tab:recovery}),
and Llama's surviving generations at $T{=}1.3$ are \emph{shorter} than at $T{=}0.7$
(90th percentile $\times 0.73$ on GSM8K, $\times 0.55$ on MMLU-Pro). Shortening is the
survivorship of Section~\ref{sec:degeneration} at work: when derailment is permanent and
its per-token risk compounds, the generations that still finish are disproportionately
the short ones. In every model, a generation that derails does not come back.

That per-token risk can be estimated from the probe itself. From the recorded top-20
arrays we compute, for each position, the probability that sampling at $T{=}1.3$ picks a
token from beyond the model's own top 20. We call this the escape hazard: the per-token probability of a
genuine tail draw. Llama's is 0.0033 per position and Mistral's 0.0034, about 0.85
expected tail draws per 256 positions for both, and across the seven models the hazard
ranks the drops at the same $\rho = +0.96$. The two models therefore take their first
off-distribution token equally often, and temperature scaling does not reveal a
difference the raw probabilities hid. What separates Llama from Mistral must then be
what happens after an off-distribution token has entered the model's own context.

A perturbation experiment measures that directly. For each model and frozen prompt we
greedy-decode a 48-token prefix, replace the next token with the model's own rank-20
candidate at that position (median raw probability $2 \times 10^{-9}$, a genuine tail
draw), and continue greedily for 256 tokens, alongside a control continuation of the
unedited prefix. Judged by the text alone, the forced token is absorbed: the
continuations stay locally coherent, and Llama's reach the token cap no more often than
its controls (14.0\% for both on GSM8K). The instability appears in the escape hazard
over the 32 positions after the injection: Llama's rises to 2.3 times its baseline
($+0.0042$ per position), Mistral's to 1.35 times ($+0.0012$), and no robust model
gains more than $+0.0014$. This per-model destabilization ranks the temperature drops
at $\rho = +0.86$ (the entropy increase, $+0.82$). It is the compounding loop
anticipated in the introduction, measured directly: an off-distribution token in
context raises the probability of drawing the next one, and the size of that rise is
what distinguishes the fragile models (Appendix~\ref{app:mechanism} consolidates the
per-model probe numbers). With seven models these are consistency checks, not a
confirmed causal account, and we report them as such.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\textbf{For practitioners.} At deployment temperatures the consequential choice is the
model. On a robust model every configuration we test stays within a few points of every
other, so time spent tuning the sampler buys little; on a fragile model the same sweep
spans up to 42 points. Screening for
the fragility is cheap, because the collapse is large and needs no judge to detect:
fifty items under plain temperature at the top of the deployment band, and a fragile
model reveals itself in the strict-parse rate alone. In production, temperature breaks
termination and leaves answer selection
intact, so a derailed generation carries no well-formed final answer and simple output
validation catches it, while the rate of silent errors, well-formed but wrong answers,
barely moves.

\textbf{For the sampler literature.} Truncation samplers solve a real problem, and the
problem sits above the temperatures at which models are deployed. The papers
introducing min-$p$ and top-$n\sigma$ validate them at $T \ge 1.5$, past every cliff we
map, so their reported gains are real safety margin beyond the cliff; our grid shows
that below the cliff there is no failure left for a sampler to prevent. A sampler
evaluation should therefore say where its models' cliffs sit, since a gain measured
past one model's cliff need not exist on a model whose cliff lies further out.

\textbf{For the robustness line.} The fragility follows a lineage: three Llama
generations produce the three largest MMLU-Pro
drops in the study while models older, newer, smaller, and larger stay robust, which points at
something the family's post-training recipe shares. Our contribution to that question
is the behavioral signature a mechanism study should explain: termination failure with
intact answer selection, first off-distribution tokens that arrive at the same
per-token rate in every model, and a post-injection destabilization that only the
fragile family shows. Which post-training choices set the size of that destabilization
is, to us, the most interesting open question the data raise.

\section{Conclusion}
Across nine models from five families, the choice of truncation sampler is a
second-order effect at deployment temperatures: robust models hold within a few points
under every sampler we test, while the Llama family loses up to 41 points under plain
temperature across three of its generations. Every model has a cliff above its
deployment range, robustness is where
the cliff sits, and truncation samplers move the cliff without mattering below it.
These results argue for screening temperature fragility before deployment rather than
tuning the sampler after it. The open question our data sharpen but cannot answer is
what, in post-training, moves the cliff.

\section*{Limitations}

Our sample is 50 items per task with $K{=}3$ repetitions per cell; the small item count
is what makes a factorial of about 204{,}000 generations feasible. Item-clustered
bootstrap CIs accompany every estimate, and the headline effects are 5 to 10 times their
CI widths. Mistral-7B sits near floor on GSM8K (32.7\% at $T{=}0.7$), which limits
collapse-detection power in that cell; its MMLU-Pro drop is measured away from floor and
its CI excludes zero. The frozen MMLU-Pro subset is single-category (business) because
the test split is sorted by category; the stratified $N{=}50$ replication of
Appendix~\ref{app:strat} reproduces the headline result.

The scope is open-weight instruction-tuned models of 1.7B to 12B parameters, served
through one inference engine. Whether larger or proprietary models share the Llama
family's fragility is untested. Both tasks are English, zero-shot, and objective: a
single exactly checkable answer is what lets the parser measure degeneration. The
original use case of truncation samplers, open-ended generation with no checkable
answer, lies outside this design, so our null result on sampler gains does not extend to
it. Quantization covers llama.cpp k-quants only, one recipe by design so that the
quantization axis varies precision and nothing else; other recipes, and other engines'
sampler implementations, may behave differently. Thinking mode is off throughout the
main grid; the thinking-on spot-check of Appendix~\ref{app:thinking} agrees with it.

Every fragile model we observe is a Llama. The lineage cell of
Section~\ref{sec:modelbound} shows the fragility spans three generations, so it is a
family trait rather than a quirk of one checkpoint, but with a single fragile family we
cannot say how common such families are.

Three analysis caveats. The max-minus-min sampler spread is biased upward, so it
overstates the differences between samplers; the bias is conservative for our claim that
those differences are small. Accuracy conditional on a strict parse carries item
survivorship, which Section~\ref{sec:degeneration} quantifies and normalizes. The
mechanism probes correlate seven models, and Section~\ref{sec:mechanism} presents them
as consistency checks rather than a causal account.

\section{Reproducibility}
\label{app:repro}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Inference stack.} llama.cpp at commit \texttt{5aba5364} (server build
    9456), native \texttt{/completion} endpoint, CUDA builds of the same commit per GPU
    architecture (sm\_75 for RTX 2080~Ti, sm\_120 for RTX 5070~Ti). Server-side BOS
    insertion is disabled; the client-rendered chat template owns the special tokens, and
    each record stores the SHA-256 of the exact rendered prompt.
  \item \textbf{Quantizations.} Every quantized checkpoint is a Bartowski imatrix GGUF
    (one provider, one recipe, so the quantization axis is not confounded by recipe).
    SHA-256 hashes of all 29 model files are pinned in the repository
    (\texttt{DOWNLOADS.md} and per-file \texttt{.meta.json} sidecars).
  \item \textbf{Decoding.} Exactly one truncation parameter per request; the sampler
    chain order is pinned per arm and recorded in every record. Sampler values: top-$p$
    0.95, top-$k$ 40, min-$p$ 0.05, top-$n\sigma$ 1.0 (each method's recommended value).
  \item \textbf{Seeding.} Every generation's seed is derived deterministically from
    (global seed 0, model, quantization, sampler, temperature, task, item, repetition).
    Greedy decoding is the deterministic $T{=}0$ anchor with $K{=}1$.
  \item \textbf{Tasks.} GSM8K (openai/gsm8k, test split, first 50 items) and MMLU-Pro
    (TIGER-Lab/MMLU-Pro, test split, first 50 items; stratified replication in
    Appendix~\ref{app:strat}). Parsers mirror lm-eval-harness; parse failures grade as
    incorrect and are reported.
  \item \textbf{Records.} Each generation is one JSONL record carrying the full identity
    tuple, the request parameters, the raw output, the parse path, and throughput
    covariates; runs are resumable by record id. Batched serving ($\le 8$ slots) is
    seed-logged but not bit-exact.

\end{itemize}
