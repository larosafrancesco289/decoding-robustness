\title{Temperature Fragility and the Conditional Benefits of Truncation Sampling}

\begin{abstract}
Truncation samplers such as top-$p$, min-$p$, and top-$n\sigma$ are meant to keep sampled
text coherent at high temperature, and their reported accuracy gains come from temperatures of
1.5 to 3. We ask when they change accuracy on tasks with a checkable answer. In one controlled
pipeline we test thirteen open-weight instruction-tuned models on GSM8K and MMLU-Pro at
temperatures 0.7, 1.0, and 1.3, seven of them under eight decoding configurations and four
quantization levels. Temperature sensitivity differs sharply by model. Between 0.7 and 1.3,
six of the thirteen lose 17 to 38 accuracy points on MMLU-Pro under plain temperature sampling
and the other seven lose at most 10. The loss consists of generations that never reach an
answer; the share of wrong answers does not rise. The Llama-3.2 collapse replicates at native
precision through a second inference engine. Truncation gains track these losses. Where plain
temperature holds its accuracy, no rule gains more than 3.3 points over 96 comparisons
(simultaneous 95\% upper bound 7.3). Where it collapses, every rule recovers most of the loss,
and on a temperature ladder to 2.0 truncation delays each model's collapse. At default
temperatures the consequential choice is the model; the sampler matters once the model has
begun to fail.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Sampling is the default decoding choice for deployed language models \citep{shi2024decoding}.
At each step the decoder draws the next token from the model's predicted distribution, and a
temperature parameter scales the randomness of the draw (greedy decoding, the deterministic
alternative, always takes the most probable token). A higher temperature moves probability
toward the tail of the distribution, the tokens with low predicted probability, where the
model's estimates are least reliable \citep{holtzman2020curious,hewitt2022truncation}.
Truncation samplers delete that tail before the draw. They were designed for open-ended
generation \citep{zhou2024decoding}, and they are now also evaluated on tasks with one
checkable answer: min-$p$ reports accuracy gains on GSM8K \citep{nguyen2024minp}, and
top-$n\sigma$ reports accuracy maintained up to a temperature of 3.0 \citep{tang2024topn}.

That evidence has two gaps. It comes from very few models, top-$n\sigma$'s from Llama-3-8B
\citep{tang2024topn} and min-$p$'s from Mistral-7B \citep{nguyen2024minp}, and it comes from
temperatures of 1.5 to 3, while deployed systems set defaults between 0.6 and 1.0
(Section~\ref{sec:design}). A reanalysis that gave every sampler the same tuning budget found
min-$p$'s gains indistinguishable from the other rules' \citep{schaeffer2025minp}. Whether a
truncation rule changes accuracy at the temperatures systems use, and for which models, has
not been measured.

We measure it. In one pipeline with fixed items, prompts, token budgets, and parser, we run
thirteen open-weight instruction-tuned models from the Llama-3, Mistral, Qwen, Gemma, and OLMo
lineages on GSM8K and MMLU-Pro at temperatures 0.7, 1.0, and 1.3. Seven models form a full grid
of eight decoding configurations and four quantization levels; six more run the temperature
test alone, and the three of them that trip a prespecified fragility flag receive the full
sampler grid. Every estimate carries an item-clustered bootstrap interval.

Three findings follow. First, temperature sensitivity differs sharply across model checkpoints
under identical conditions: six of the thirteen lose 17 to 38 points on MMLU-Pro between 0.7
and 1.3 under plain temperature sampling, and the other seven lose at most 10. The
collapse of Llama-3.2-3B replicates at native precision through a second engine, and
Hermes-3, a different post-training of the Llama-3.1-8B base, shows roughly half of
Llama-3.1's drop. Second, the loss is degeneration. In every fragile model the share of
generations that end at the token cap or contain nothing to grade rises by 26 to 78 points,
while the share of parsed wrong answers falls or stays flat. Third, truncation gains track
these losses. On the six models that hold their accuracy, no rule gains more than 3.3 points at
temperatures 0.7 and 1.0 (96 comparisons, simultaneous 95\% upper bound 7.3). On the fragile
models every rule recovers most of the loss at 1.3, and a ladder to temperature 2.0 shows
truncation delaying each model's collapse, which is the regime where the sampler papers measured
their gains. For a practitioner the order of decisions follows: choose the model and the
temperature together, because at default temperatures the sampler changes little on a model that
holds its accuracy and much on a model that has begun to lose it.

\section{Background}
\label{sec:background}

\textbf{Truncation rules.} Top-$k$ keeps the $k$ most probable tokens \citep{fan2018hierarchical}.
Nucleus sampling, or top-$p$, keeps the smallest set whose probabilities sum to $p$, so the set
grows when the distribution is flat \citep{holtzman2020curious}. Min-$p$ keeps every token whose
probability is at least a fixed fraction of the most probable token's \citep{nguyen2024minp}.
Top-$n\sigma$ works on logits (the model's scores before they are converted to probabilities)
and keeps tokens within $n$ standard deviations of the top logit; temperature rescales all logits
equally, so the retained set does not change with temperature \citep{tang2024topn}. The order of
temperature and truncation matters for the probability-space rules, because the threshold is
computed on whichever distribution the rule sees: applied after temperature it sees a flattened
distribution and keeps a different set of tokens. We run both orders. Two 2026 rules set the
threshold adaptively so that it is invariant to temperature by construction
\citep{tan2026pless,ding2026mink}; the evaluation of Min-$k$ starts at temperature 1.0, where its
GSM8K results put top-$k$, top-$p$, and min-$p$ within a point of each other, and the rules
separate only above temperature 2 \citep{ding2026mink}. \citet{welleck2020consistency} proved
that top-$k$ and nucleus decoding can produce sequences that never terminate, so
non-termination is a documented decoding failure.

\textbf{Prior evaluations.} Sampler papers compare rules against each other. Decoding surveys
report that the best method changes with the task and the model \citep{shi2024decoding}, that
the optimal configuration for open-ended generation varies by model \citep{garcesarias2025decoding},
and that the gap between greedy decoding and sampling on reasoning benchmarks differs by model
\citep{song2024goodbad}. Temperature studies stop at 1.0 or use tasks with little accuracy to
lose: \citet{renze2024temperature} find no effect between 0.0 and 1.0 on multiple-choice
problems, and \citet{grover2026reliability} conclude that instruction tuning is the main
determinant of robustness, on synthetic tasks where the robust models score 11 to 30\% and with
no truncation rule tested. \citet{fastowski2025confidence} link token-distribution entropy to
temperature sensitivity on factual questions, and \citet{troshin2025temperature} attribute
high-temperature losses on mathematics to wrong tokens at a few critical positions. Two
recent studies motivate our design choices: benchmark scores depend on the inference backend
even under greedy decoding \citep{masoudian2026backend}, so we use one engine and disclose it,
and quantization and temperature do not compound in a factorial study of eight models
\citep{prasad2026quantization}, which we check on our own grid (Appendix~\ref{app:temp}).
None of these studies asks the question we ask: whether a model loses accuracy at the
temperatures systems use, and whether a truncation rule changes that.

\label{sec:design}

\textbf{Decoding.} Eight configurations: greedy decoding, plain temperature sampling, and the
four truncation rules of Section~\ref{sec:background} at the setting each source paper uses
(top-$p$ 0.95 \citep{holtzman2020curious}, top-$k$ 40 \citep{radford2019gpt2}, min-$p$ 0.05
\citep{nguyen2024minp}, top-$n\sigma$ 1.0 \citep{tang2024topn}), with temperature applied before
truncation, plus two ablations that apply top-$p$ and min-$p$ after temperature. Temperatures
0.7 and 1.0 bracket the defaults that models and engines set (Llama-3.1's generation
configuration sets 0.6, llama.cpp 0.8, the OpenAI and Anthropic APIs 1.0),\footnote{Sources:
Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct's \texttt{generation\_config.json}; llama.cpp's defaults; the OpenAI and
Anthropic API references, July 2026.} and 1.3 is a stress point above them. Exactly one
truncation parameter is sent per request and the engine instantiates only that rule
(Appendix~\ref{app:repro}).

\textbf{Tasks and grading.} GSM8K, grade-school word problems \citep{cobbe2021gsm8k}, with the
zero-shot chain-of-thought prompt of Meta's Llama evaluation suite \citep{meta2024llamaevals} and
numeric exact match; and MMLU-Pro, ten-option multiple choice \citep{wang2024mmlupro}, zero-shot
chain of thought and letter exact match. Both need a chain of reasoning ending in one exactly
checkable answer, and MMLU-Pro's reasoning runs about twice as long. The token budgets are 512
on GSM8K and 1024 on MMLU-Pro; a generation that reaches its budget is \emph{capped}. Parsers
follow lm-eval-harness \citep{gao2023evalharness} and log one of three outcomes: \emph{strict},
the pinned final-answer sentence is present (``The final answer is 26''; ``The answer is
(I)''); \emph{flexible}, the sentence is absent and the parser falls back to the last number or
the last parenthesized letter; \emph{failed}, neither exists, graded incorrect.
Appendix~\ref{app:grading} shows one generation per outcome. Degenerate text usually contains
a number, so on GSM8K an incoherent generation is graded as a wrong answer through the flexible
path, while on MMLU-Pro it fails to parse; the capped flag records the failure on both.

\textbf{Models.} The main grid holds seven instruction-tuned models from five families:
Llama-3.1-8B \citep{grattafiori2024llama}, Mistral-7B-v0.3 \citep{jiang2023mistral}, Qwen2.5-7B
\citep{yang2024qwen25}, Qwen3-1.7B, 4B, and 8B \citep{yang2025qwen3} with thinking off, and
Gemma-3-12B \citep{gemma2025gemma3}, each at four GGUF quantization levels (Q8\_0, Q6\_K,
Q4\_K\_M, Q3\_K\_M) from one provider and one recipe. Q8\_0 is the primary precision for every
temperature result; the sampler bound of Section~\ref{sec:bound} pools the four levels.
The temperature extension adds six models at Q8\_0: two further Llama-3 releases, Llama-3-8B
\citep{meta2024llama3} and Llama-3.2-3B \citep{meta2024llama32}; Hermes-3-8B
\citep{teknium2024hermes3}, a different post-training of the Llama-3.1-8B base; Qwen3.5-9B
\citep{qwen2026qwen35} and Gemma-4-E4B \citep{google2026gemma4}, two widely used 2026 releases;
and OLMo-3-7B \citep{ai2025olmo3}, a fully open model. The four 2026 models need a later
llama.cpp release than the grid's pinned build, so two grid models rerun on that release as a
bridge (Section~\ref{sec:sensitivity}).

\textbf{Fragility flag.} Before the extension ran, we fixed a flag: a plain-temperature accuracy
drop of at least 15 points from 0.7 to 1.3, a capped-rate rise of at least 20, or a strict-parse
fall of at least 20, on either task. A flagged model receives the full eight-configuration grid
at Q8\_0. Three of the four 2026-panel models tripped it (Hermes-3, Qwen3.5-9B, OLMo-3-7B).

\textbf{Engine and precision.} Every grid result comes from llama.cpp on quantized weights, so
the collapse could in principle belong to the engine or the conversion. The engine check reruns
the temperature test through HF transformers on the official BF16 weights of Llama-3.2-3B, in
8-bit weights for Llama-3.1-8B (the most a 16\,GB card holds), and in BF16 for Qwen3-4B as a
robust control, plus Llama-3.2-3B on its unquantized F16 GGUF through llama.cpp.

\textbf{Inference.} Every interval is a 95\% nonparametric bootstrap clustered on the 50 items
($B{=}2000$; 5000 for the sampler bound), paired because both arms of every comparison ran the
same items. Item accuracy is the mean over repetitions, and over quantization levels where
levels are pooled. The sampler bound is a family-wise statement: over a stated set of paired
comparisons, the 95th percentile of the bootstrap distribution of the largest mean gain. A
generalized estimating equation over the same records reproduces every interval to within
0.6 points (Appendix~\ref{app:sampler}). Hardware, the pinned builds, model hashes, and the
anonymized code and records are in Appendix~\ref{app:repro}.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Temperature sensitivity differs across checkpoints}
\label{sec:sensitivity}

Figure~\ref{fig:drops} gives the plain-temperature accuracy drop from 0.7 to 1.3 for all
thirteen models at Q8\_0, and Appendix~\ref{app:temp} the accuracies at every temperature.
Six models lose 17 to 38 points on MMLU-Pro: Llama-3.1-8B, Llama-3.2-3B, Qwen3.5-9B,
Hermes-3-8B, OLMo-3-7B, and Llama-3-8B. The other seven lose at most 10, and their intervals all
include 4 or less. The two Llama checkpoints with the largest drops lose 30 and 38 points on
GSM8K as well; on the shorter task the other four fragile models lose 4 to 12, so the flag
fires on MMLU-Pro only. Llama-3.1's decline has begun by temperature 1.0, where its MMLU-Pro
accuracy is already 10 points below its 0.7 level. Neither headroom nor age explains the split:
Qwen3-1.7B starts above Llama-3.1 on GSM8K and does not collapse, and Mistral-7B-v0.3 predates
Llama-3.1 and is robust.

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.92\textwidth]{fig1_drops}
\caption{Accuracy drop under plain temperature sampling from $T{=}0.7$ to $1.3$ at Q8\_0, with
95\% item-clustered bootstrap intervals; the accuracies at the two temperatures are printed at
the right. Models are sorted by the MMLU-Pro drop; the six above the dotted line lose at least 15
points on one task.}
\label{fig:drops}
\end{figure*}

The collapse is a property of the checkpoint under fixed conditions, and it survives a change of
engine. Through transformers on its official BF16 weights, Llama-3.2-3B loses 40.0 points on
GSM8K and 36.0 on MMLU-Pro (Q8\_0 through llama.cpp: 38.0 and 36.7), with 61 and 92\% of its
generations capped at 1.3; the paired differences between the two engines' drops are $+2.0$
[$-8.7$, 12.0] and $-0.7$ [$-9.3$, 8.7]. Llama-3.1-8B in 8-bit weights loses 43.3 and 42.7, and
Qwen3-4B in BF16 moves by 1.3 and $-0.7$, as in its GGUF cells. These runs replicate the
collapse; they do not show that the engines are equivalent, since the intervals on the
differences span about 20 points and the 8B GSM8K difference excludes zero
(Appendix~\ref{app:repro}). The two grid models rerun on the later llama.cpp release reproduce
their pinned-build drops with differences that include zero, and the stratified MMLU-Pro subset
reproduces the ordering (Llama-3.1 $+43.3$ [31.3, 55.3]; Qwen2.5 $+6.0$; Gemma-3 $+7.3$).

Hermes-3-8B shares the Llama-3.1-8B base and shows roughly half of its MMLU-Pro drop (19.3
against 38.0), with the same pattern of capped generations. Post-training changed the size of
the collapse on one base; the design cannot say which part of it did. The six fragile
checkpoints span three lineages, Llama-derived, Qwen, and OLMo, but the three Meta releases
share data and methods, so they are not three independent observations.

\subsection{The loss is capped or unparseable generations}
\label{sec:degeneration}

This subsection uses plain temperature sampling throughout. An accuracy drop can come from two
failures, completed answers that are wrong or generations that never reach an answer, and the
parser fields separate them. Figure~\ref{fig:outcomes} splits every MMLU-Pro generation into
three exclusive outcomes: correct; capped or unparseable (the generation reached its token
budget, or contained no answer to grade); and answered wrong (a parsed answer that is wrong
and ended before the budget). In all six fragile models the capped-or-unparseable share rises
between 0.7 and 1.3, by 26 to 78 points, and the answered-wrong share falls or stays flat
(Llama-3.1: 31 to 3; Hermes-3: 49 to 29; Qwen3.5: 9 to 9). In the seven robust models the
capped share moves by at most 11 points. GSM8K shows the same pattern for the two Llama
checkpoints (Appendix~\ref{app:grading}).

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{fig2_outcomes}
\caption{Outcome of every MMLU-Pro generation under plain temperature sampling at Q8\_0, three
bars per model for $T{=}0.7$, 1.0, and 1.3. Categories are exclusive, in this precedence:
correct; capped or unparseable; answered wrong. Model order as in Figure~\ref{fig:drops}.}
\label{fig:outcomes}
\end{figure}

What the capped generations contain differs by temperature. A blinded audit of 152 sampled
outputs, labelled by a language-model annotator that saw only the task and the raw text,
found every Llama generation capped at 1.3 to be incoherent token noise, every Llama generation
capped at 0.7 to be coherent reasoning cut off by the budget, and most parse failures at 0.7 to
be formatting failures; 34 of the 152 labels were marked borderline (Appendix~\ref{app:grading}).
Budget censoring is therefore visible at 0.7 in the verbose models, OLMo-3 and Qwen3.5 already
cap 44 and 29\% of their MMLU-Pro generations there, and the rise from 0.7 to 1.3 is the
degeneration signal. Qwen3.5's degenerate streams often stop before the budget, so its capped
rate understates its failure and its strict-parse rate carries it (a fall of 31 points).

Among the generations that do complete with the pinned final-answer sentence, accuracy at 1.3 is
subject to selection, because easier items produce shorter generations that finish before
losing coherence. The matched comparison is greedy decoding on the same items. On MMLU-Pro,
Llama-3.1's completed answers score 78.9\% against 78.9\% for matched greedy, Qwen3.5's 83.1
against 84.7, and OLMo-3's 73.3 against 73.3; for Llama-3-8B, Llama-3.2-3B (nine survivors), and
Hermes-3 the completed answers score below matched greedy, by 4, 11, and 16 points
(Appendix~\ref{app:grading}). Temperature lowers the rate at which every fragile model finishes,
and in three of the six it also lowers the accuracy of what finishes.

\subsection{Measured sampler gains are small where plain temperature holds}
\label{sec:bound}

This subsection varies the sampler. For each model, task, and temperature we compare each
truncation rule with plain temperature on the same items (all contrasts in
Appendix~\ref{app:sampler}). Table~\ref{tab:bound} summarizes the family of comparisons behind
each claim. On the six grid models that hold their accuracy, at temperatures 0.7 and 1.0 and
pooled over quantization, the largest gain over 96 comparisons is 3.3 points, the simultaneous
95\% upper bound on the largest gain is 7.3, and 2 intervals lie above zero against 3 below.
Restricted to Q8\_0 the point estimates stay under 5 and the bound widens to about 13. On the
same six models at 1.3, gains reach 9 points and 13 of 48 intervals lie above zero, on
Mistral, Qwen2.5, and Gemma-3, the three with the largest drops among the robust seven.
Llama-3.1 is different already at 1.0: on MMLU-Pro, where its plain-temperature accuracy has
fallen 10 points, min-$p$ and top-$n\sigma$ gain 11 points (pooled), and on the flagged models'
Q8\_0 grids Hermes-3 gains 8 with top-$n\sigma$ at the same temperature. The order of
temperature and truncation changes accuracy by at most 4 points on the grid models at
$T \le 1.0$; on Llama-3.1 at 1.3, truncating before temperature scaling keeps 22 more points
under top-$p$ on MMLU-Pro, because the rule then cuts the sharper distribution
(Appendix~\ref{app:sampler}).

fragile models, thinking mode, or open-ended generation.

\subsection{Truncation recovers accuracy as plain temperature loses it}
\label{sec:recovery}

Across the 60 model, task, and temperature combinations with a sampler grid (seven grid models
at Q8\_0 and three flagged models), the gain of the best rule over plain temperature rises with
the loss of plain temperature against greedy decoding (Pearson $r{=}0.89$; 0.78 over all four
rules), a descriptive association plotted in Appendix~\ref{app:sampler}. Figure~\ref{fig:recovery}
gives the estimates that carry it, for the four fragile checkpoints with a grid at temperature
1.3. Every rule gains on every model and task, and 20 of the 24 intervals on the flagged models
exclude zero. Against the plain-temperature accuracy at 0.7 (right panel), the best rule finishes
within six points in all eight model-task cells, in point estimates: top-$n\sigma$ returns
Llama-3.1's MMLU-Pro accuracy from 10.0 to 52.0\% and Qwen3.5's from 38.0 to 59.3\%, and min-$p$
returns Hermes-3's from 26.0 to 46.0\% and OLMo-3's from 26.7 to 42.7\%. The recovery is not
uniform: top-$k$ leaves Hermes-3 8 points below its 0.7 level on MMLU-Pro, min-$p$ leaves OLMo-3
11 points below on GSM8K, and top-$p$ leaves Llama-3.1 31 points below on MMLU-Pro. Every rule
was compared with plain temperature only, and we do not rank the rules.


\textbf{Where each model collapses.} To see whether the robust models also collapse, the ladder
run extends the temperature to 2.0 on Llama-3.1-8B, Qwen2.5-7B, and Gemma-3-12B under four
configurations (Figure~\ref{fig:ladder}; drops with intervals in Appendix~\ref{app:ladder}).
Take the collapse temperature as the lowest tested temperature at which a configuration loses
more than half of the model's accuracy at 0.7. Under plain temperature the three models collapse
on GSM8K at 1.5, 1.7, and 2.0, in the order of Figure~\ref{fig:drops}, and at 1.5 Llama's drop
interval is disjoint from the other two models'. On MMLU-Pro, the longer task, each collapses
one step earlier (1.3, 1.5, 1.5). Truncation moves the collapse later, and the more of the tail
a rule removes the further it moves it: top-$p$ by one step, min-$p$ to 2.0 on Llama's GSM8K and
past 2.0 on the other two models, and top-$n\sigma$ past 2.0 on every model and task, so its
collapse temperature lies outside the tested range. Min-$p$ and top-$n\sigma$ were validated at
temperatures of 1.5 to 3 \citep{nguyen2024minp,tang2024topn}, on the far side of every collapse
we measure, which is where their gains are largest here too.


\section{Discussion and conclusion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The three findings fit one description. Under plain temperature sampling, some checkpoints keep
their accuracy well above the default temperatures and others lose it by 1.3, and the loss is
degeneration: generations stop terminating. A truncation rule removes the tail draws that
start the degeneration, so it recovers accuracy in proportion to what plain temperature loses,
and where nothing is lost it has nothing to recover. This reconciles the sampler literature with
the small gains we measure at default temperatures. Min-$p$ and top-$n\sigma$ were evaluated
above every collapse temperature we observe, and there they help; Min-$k$'s own comparison at
temperature 1.0 shows the probability-based rules within a point of each other
\citep{ding2026mink}, as ours does.

For a practitioner running objective tasks, the two settings should be chosen together. On a
model that holds its accuracy at the intended temperature, our data put the accuracy gain from a
standard truncation rule under 8 points and the point estimates under 4. On a model that loses
accuracy there, either a lower temperature or a truncation rule recovers most of the loss, and
the fragility is visible before deployment: the rise in capped generations and the fall in
strict-parse rate between 0.7 and 1.3 separate the fragile models from the robust ones in every
run of this study, including the panel run after the flag was written. We measure accuracy
only, so we say nothing about the output diversity a higher temperature buys.

For sampler evaluations, a gain should be reported next to the plain-temperature loss it
recovers, since a gain measured past one model's collapse temperature need not exist on a model
whose collapse lies further out. What moves the collapse temperature remains open. Hermes-3 shows
that post-training changes its size on one base, and the grid contains no base-versus-instruct
or checkpoint axis, so the cause is not identified here.

\section*{Limitations}

Each task has 50 fixed items with $K{=}3$ repetitions; the small item count is what makes a
factorial run of 185,600 generations feasible, and every main estimate carries an item-clustered
interval. The fixed MMLU-Pro subset is single-category (business) because the test split is
sorted by category; the stratified subset reproduces the ordering on 50 fresh items and is a
second sample of 50, so neither estimates the benchmark-wide effect. Mistral-7B's GSM8K accuracy
is 31\% at 0.7, which limits the power to detect a further drop there.

Temperature 1.3 is a stress point above the defaults, chosen to bound the deployment range from
above. The token budgets are fixed, and budget censoring is visible at 0.7 on the verbose
models; the audit distinguishes cut-off reasoning from degenerate text on a sample of 152
outputs, labelled by a language model with 34 borderline cases, and the remaining records are
unaudited. The
survivor comparison bounds the selection effect with matched greedy decoding and does not remove
it.

The sampler evidence is narrower than the temperature evidence. Thirteen models ran the
temperature test; ten ran a sampler grid, and the three flagged models were selected by their
temperature drop, so the recovery estimates describe a fragility-defined subgroup. ``Best rule''
is chosen after the fact, which favours the recovery. Rules were tested at one setting each,
in one engine, and the bound applies to those settings. The two engine checks replicate the
collapse for two Llama checkpoints and one control; Llama-3.1's check ran in 8-bit weights, its
native precision untested, and the intervals on the between-engine differences are about 20 points wide.
Repeated generations, temperatures, and rules are not independent observations, so the
gain-versus-loss correlation is descriptive.

The scope is open-weight instruction-tuned models of 1.7B to 12B parameters, in English,
zero-shot, on objective tasks with one checkable answer, with thinking mode off. A spot-check
of Qwen3-8B with thinking on shows a 10-point MMLU-Pro drop [2, 20] at $N{=}25$
(Appendix~\ref{app:temp}), so the bound does not cover thinking mode. Open-ended generation, the
original use of truncation samplers, lies outside the design, as does the diversity a higher
temperature buys under multi-sample selection. The study identifies no cause for the
differences between checkpoints.

\section{Reproducibility and engine checks}
\label{app:repro}

\textbf{Inference stack.} llama.cpp at commit \texttt{5aba5364} (server build 9456), native
\texttt{/completion} endpoint, CUDA builds per GPU architecture (sm\_75 for an RTX 2080~Ti,
sm\_120 for an RTX 5070~Ti). Server-side BOS insertion is disabled; the client-rendered chat
template owns the special tokens, and each record stores the SHA-256 of the rendered prompt.
The 2026 panel, its sampler grids, and the bridge run use release v0.4.0 (commit
\texttt{5266f24d}), which two panel models require, with the same client-side rendering and the
server's template parser off. That release forces its own BOS for Gemma~4, so the Gemma-4 prompt
is rendered without one; it also reports the cap-length degenerate streams that the pinned build
returned with a zero token count as an HTTP error without text, and the runner records those as
zero-token capped generations in both builds. Every quantized checkpoint is a Bartowski imatrix
GGUF; SHA-256 hashes of the model files are in the repository (\texttt{DOWNLOADS.md}).

\textbf{Decoding and seeding.} Exactly one truncation parameter per request; the chain order is
pinned through the server's \texttt{samplers} array and recorded in every record. At both builds
the server instantiates only the samplers named in that array, so its defaults for unlisted
samplers (top-$k$ 40, top-$p$ 0.95, min-$p$ 0.05) are inert, and no penalty, DRY, XTC, or
logit-bias parameter is sent. Every generation's seed is derived from (global seed 0, model,
quantization, sampler, temperature, task, item, repetition). Batched serving ($\le 8$ slots) is
seed-logged but not bit-exact. Tasks: GSM8K (openai/gsm8k, test split, first 50 items) and
MMLU-Pro (TIGER-Lab/MMLU-Pro, test split, first 50 items). Each generation is one JSONL record
with the identity tuple, request parameters, raw output, parse outcome, and token counts; runs
are resumable by record id. The pipeline, configurations, analysis scripts, and every record are
at \url{https://anonymous.4open.science/r/decoding-robustness-027C}.

\textbf{Engine check.} The transformers configuration loads \texttt{meta-llama/Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct}
(revision \texttt{0cb88a4f}) in BF16 with transformers 5.16.1 and torch 2.14, samples with
\texttt{do\_sample} at temperature $T$ with top-$k$ and top-$p$ disabled and no other processor,
in batches of eight with left padding; greedy decoding is \texttt{do\_sample=False}. Generation
stops on the model's end-of-sequence set or at the task's budget. The prompt is rendered from
the same template as the grid; the template stamps the run date into the Llama system header,
the only difference between the runs' prompt strings, and the prompt token counts agree on every
item. Llama-3.1-8B loads the \texttt{unsloth/Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct} mirror of the official
weights (revision \texttt{4699cc75}) in 8-bit through bitsandbytes, in batches of four; Qwen3-4B
loads \texttt{Qwen/Qwen3-4B} (revision \texttt{1cfa9a72}) in BF16 with thinking disabled. Both
reproduce every grid prompt hash. Table~\ref{tab:engine-full} gives accuracy levels, drops,
capped rates, and the paired difference of each configuration's drop from the pinned Q8\_0
llama.cpp cell, including the v0.4.0 bridge rows.

\section{Grading and degeneration}

Figure~\ref{fig:parsepaths} shows one generation per parse outcome and Figure~\ref{fig:degenexamples}

after it. The flexible rule catches two different things: a well-formed answer written outside
the pinned sentence, and an incoherent generation that still contains a number, which GSM8K
